Create an account

An account is optional and it is not a paywall — there is nothing behind it that you cannot already do. It exists for two situations: you study on a laptop and revise on a phone, and two people share one machine or one deployment. Your progress so far comes with you.

📌 The last screen matters more than this one

When you press the button, you will be shown a recovery code once and never again. It is the only way back into the account if you forget your password, because this service has no mail transport and therefore no reset email — and a reset that verifies nothing would not be a reset, it would be a back door.

Put it in your password manager beside the password. Losing both is losing the account; your work would survive, because it is also in this browser and Export a backup gives you a file you can import into a new one, but the account itself would be gone.

🧒 What the school actually writes down

Imagine a school that keeps one folder per pupil. In yours there is the name you chose, every test you have ever taken, and — instead of your password — a scrambled version of it that cannot be un-scrambled. When you come back and say the password, they scramble what you said the same way and check that the two scribbles match. There is no address in the folder, because the school never sends anything.

So a thief who steals the whole filing cabinet still cannot walk into your house. They have the scribble, not the word. And because the scrambling is deliberately slow, guessing a million words to find the right one takes them a very long afternoon.

🎓 What gets stored, exactly

Five columns on the user row: the normalised username, the display name, a PBKDF2 hash with its per-user salt and its iteration count, and a SHA-256 of the recovery code. No email address — it could never be verified, never be written to and never be used to recover anything, so collecting one would be personal data held for no purpose, which is what data minimisation exists to stop.

The salt is why two people who pick the same password get different hashes, so one cracked hash does not crack the other. The iteration count is stored per row rather than as a global constant, which is what lets the cost be raised later without invalidating everyone: on your next successful sign-in the plaintext is momentarily available again, and the row is re-hashed at the new cost.

The recovery code gets a plain SHA-256 where the password gets PBKDF2, and that is not an oversight. Slow hashing buys time only against a low-entropy, human-chosen secret; the code is 100 bits from a cryptographic RNG, so nothing guesses it however fast the hash runs. Being able to say which of those two applies, and why, is most of that interview question.

Alongside it sits your progress document — one JSON blob, stored verbatim. The server parses it only to pull out the leaderboard figures, and only because a number shown next to other people's names should not be one the browser simply asserted.

Read the code: src/LogiFlow.Academy.Api/Security/PasswordHasher.cs and Endpoints/AuthEndpoints.cs, then the chapter it comes from — 15 · Securing an API.

⚠ The one place enumeration is not the answer

If the name is taken, this form says so plainly — unlike sign in and recovery, which are careful never to admit whether an account exists. That looks like an inconsistency and is a deliberate line.

The enumeration argument only bites when the identifier is private. An email address is: learning that you@work.it has an account here is a disclosure about you. A username on this site is not — it is printed on the leaderboard for anyone to read. So refusing to say “taken” would protect nothing while leaving every person who picks a common name guessing why registration failed. Know which of those two you are holding before copying either behaviour.
