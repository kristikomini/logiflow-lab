Your CV, LinkedIn and this project

Fifty-two chapters of technical preparation are worth nothing until somebody decides to talk to you. That decision is made in about thirty seconds, by a person who is not a developer, looking at a PDF and a LinkedIn profile. This chapter is about those thirty seconds.

Why this chapter exists

It is the one part of the process where a small amount of effort changes the outcome disproportionately — and the one part almost nobody practises. Italian conventions also differ from the Anglo-Saxon ones in ways that get a CV quietly filtered out, and most juniors present a portfolio in the way that helps them least.

What actually happens to your CV

🧒 Simple

Imagine a teacher with two hundred drawings to look through and ten minutes. They are not studying each one. They are flicking, and stopping only when something catches the eye.

So the question is not "is my drawing good?" It is "in the two seconds before they flick past, is there something that makes them stop?"

🎓 Professional

Three filters, in this order, and each has a different reader:

  1. A keyword match, often automated. Large consultancies use applicant tracking systems that score your CV against the advert's terms. This is why the CV should contain the advert's actual words — C#, .NET Core, T-SQL, SQL Server, Web API, Entity Framework Core — written plainly, not as a graphic. A skills bar chart or a two-column PDF full of text boxes often parses to nothing.
  2. A recruiter's scan. Not a developer. Looking for: is this person real, are they close enough to the office, can they work here legally, do the words in the advert appear, and is there anything concrete.
  3. A developer's read, and only now does the content matter. They are looking for one thing they could ask you about for ten minutes.

Optimise for all three by being specific and plain. Nothing on a technical CV should require interpretation.

The only bullet formula you need

🎓 Professional

Almost every junior CV bullet describes presence: "worked on the backend", "used C# and SQL". That tells a reader you were in the room. Replace it with three parts: what you did → with what → and what changed. 

✗  Sviluppo backend con C# e SQL Server.

✓  Costruita una Web API in ASP.NET Core (.NET 10) su SQL Server con EF Core:
   28 endpoint, autenticazione JWT, 120 test automatici.
   Ridotto il tempo della query di ricerca ordini da 4 s a 90 ms
   aggiungendo un indice non-clustered e riscrivendo un filtro non-SARGable.
Line by line
  • Sviluppo backend con C# e SQL Server — true of a thousand candidates, so it distinguishes nothing. It is a job title, not evidence.
  • The good version names the stack precisely — ASP.NET Core, .NET 10, EF Core, SQL Server. Those are the words the advert used, and often the words a recruiter searches.
  • 28 endpoint, 120 test automatici — numbers you can defend. Counted, not estimated, because the follow-up question is "how did you test them?"
  • da 4 s a 90 ms — a measured before and after. It is the strongest line on the page precisely because it is falsifiable.
  • indice non-clustered and filtro non-SARGable — the mechanism, not just the outcome. This one clause proves you understand chapter 11 rather than having watched a profiler.
  • It is also bait, deliberately. It invites "tell me about that index", which is a ten-minute question you can answer well — and steering the interview toward your strongest ground is a legitimate skill.
  • Write it in Italian for this market, keeping the technical nouns in English, exactly as they are used on the job.

The second one is not longer because it is padded — it is longer because it contains facts. And notice what it does: every noun in it is a question the interviewer can now ask, on ground you chose. That is the real function of a CV bullet.

Have a number wherever one honestly exists. Not invented ones — measured ones. "4 s to 90 ms" is something you saw in an execution plan in chapter 11.

Presenting a project like this one

🧒 Simple

If you show someone a big box of Lego you built, the first thing they need is a photo of the finished thing and one sentence saying what it is. Not a list of every brick.

And the thing that makes them impressed is not that it is big. It is when you say: "I built the tower this way instead of that way, because the other way fell over."

🎓 Professional

A repository is only an asset if someone can understand it in ninety seconds. Five things, in order:

  1. Pin it on your GitHub profile. An unpinned repo among thirty forks is invisible.
  2. The README is the product. First screen: one sentence on what it is, a screenshot or a diagram, the stack as a list, and how to run it in two commands. Most readers never scroll further, so everything that matters is above that fold.
  3. Make it runnable. docker compose up -d then dotnet run is worth more than a page of description. If it cannot be run in five minutes, it will not be run.
  4. Write down one decision and one rejected alternative. This is the single highest-value paragraph you can write: "the dispatcher is hand-written rather than MediatR, because …" or "the outbox exists because you cannot commit to a database and a broker atomically." Anyone can assemble a CRUD app from a tutorial; almost nobody can explain a trade-off they chose.
  5. Commit history matters more than you think. Small, well-described commits over months read as a working developer. One commit called "initial commit" containing 40 000 lines reads as something you downloaded.
⚠ What not to put on GitHub

Tutorial output with your name on it, half-finished experiments, and anything with a real connection string or key in its history. Two good repositories beat twenty; a reviewer will open the worst one. And if a secret has been committed, rotating it is the fix — deleting the line is not, as chapter 15 says.

LinkedIn, as a search index

🎓 Professional

Stop thinking of it as a profile and start thinking of it as a row in a database that recruiters query. In this region they are literally typing "C# .NET SQL Server Bologna" into a search box. Optimise for being found, not for being admired.

  • The headline is the searchable field. Not "Aspiring developer | Passionate about technology". Write "Junior .NET Developer — C#, ASP.NET Core, SQL Server | Modena". Technologies and a place.
  • Location must be the region you want, not where you happen to be. Filters are geographic and you are invisible outside them.
  • Turn on "Open to work", recruiters-only if you prefer discretion. It puts you in a filtered pool they use daily.
  • Fill the Skills section with the exact technology names. It is matched against searches directly.
  • The About section in Italian, three short paragraphs: what you build, the project with its link, what you are looking for.
  • Connect with local IT recruiters by name. A handful of agencies place most junior .NET roles in Modena and Bologna, and they work from their own network first.
L'autocandidatura

A genuinely Italian route, and an under-used one: writing directly to software houses that have no advertised opening. Many of the region's employers are small, hire when someone good appears rather than on a schedule, and never post publicly at all.

It works when it is specific and short. Name the company and something true about what they do — the sector they serve, a product on their site. Two sentences on what you build, the repository link, and a closing offer of a fifteen-minute call. Send it to a named person, not to info@. A generic message copy-pasted to forty companies is worse than not writing, because these companies talk to each other.

Try it yourself

Take the three weakest bullets on your current CV and rewrite them with the formula: what you did → with what → what changed. Then run this test on each one: 

Could an interviewer ask me a ten-minute question from this line?
Is there a number in it that I actually measured?
Does it contain a word that appears in the advert?
Line by line
  • Three questions, applied to every line of the CV. A line that fails all three is filler and should be cut.
  • "Could an interviewer ask me a ten-minute question from this line?" — if not, it carries no information. And if yes, you must be able to answer it, which is the honesty check.
  • "Is there a number in it that I actually measured?" — actually is the load-bearing word. An invented figure does not survive one follow-up question, and losing credibility costs more than the line gained.
  • "Does it contain a word that appears in the advert?" — CVs are filtered before they are read, sometimes by a person skimming and sometimes by software. Matching the advert's vocabulary is not gaming anything; it is answering the question asked.
  • Cutting is the work. One page for a junior, and every line that survives should earn its place against all three.

Any bullet that fails all three is decoration — delete it, and the CV gets better by subtraction. Then do the harder exercise: write the "one decision, one rejected alternative" paragraph for your project. If you cannot write it, you have found the thing to go and understand before the interview, which is more useful than the paragraph.

Golden rules

  1. One page. PDF. CV_Cognome_Nome.pdf. Never .docx.
  2. Plain text, one column. Graphics and skill bars parse to nothing in an ATS.
  3. Use the advert's own words for the technologies you genuinely know.
  4. Include the GDPR authorisation line. Its absence is noticed.
  5. State work authorisation explicitly if it could be in doubt.
  6. Italian for Italian employers, even imperfect. Keep an English version too.
  7. CEFR levels for languages. "Good English" is not a claim.
  8. Every bullet: what you did, with what, and what changed.
  9. Numbers you measured, never numbers you invented.
  10. Lead with the project, not the education — especially as a career changer.
  11. The README is the product. Runnable in five minutes or it will not be run.
  12. Write down one decision and one rejected alternative. It is the paragraph that gets you asked about.
  13. Your LinkedIn headline is a search field: technologies and a place.
  14. Never claim a technology you could not survive one follow-up question about.

Interview questions

"Mi parli di un progetto di cui è orgoglioso."

Ninety seconds with a shape: what it does, the one hard part, what you decided and what you rejected, and what you would change now. The last clause is the one that separates candidates — being able to criticise your own work reads as experience, and pretending it is perfect reads as inexperience.

"I see you have no professional experience. Why should we take you?"

Do not apologise, and do not oversell. Answer with evidence: you have built a real thing with the same stack they use, you can explain the decisions in it, and you have made the mistakes already — on your own time rather than on their production database. Then name the one concrete thing you would need help with in the first month, which is more convincing than confidence.

"Perché ha lasciato il suo settore precedente?"

Career changers get this always. Answer forwards, never backwards: what pulled you toward this, not what pushed you out of that. And connect the old field to the new one honestly — analysis, process, dealing with customers, a domain you understand that most developers do not. That last one is a real asset in industrial and gestionale work.

"Where do you see yourself in five years?"

They are checking that you will still be there in two. Give a direction rather than a title: deeper on the backend and the database, then enough architectural judgement to be trusted with a whole feature. Mentioning the domain — the region's manufacturing or gestionale world — reads as someone planning to stay, which is exactly what a small employer is worried about.
