Message brokers and queues

Caching relieved read pressure and search relieved query pressure. A message broker relieves something different: time. It lets work that does not have to happen now stop happening now, and it lets two services stop needing each other to be awake at the same moment.

Why this chapter exists

RabbitMQ, Kafka and Azure Service Bus travel through Italian backend adverts alongside microservizi, architetture event-driven, DDD and CQRS — from Milano consultancies to product companies in Bologna. The repository already contains the hard half: OutboxMessage and OutboxProcessor. This chapter explains what they feed.

Why not just call the other service

🧒 Simple

You want to tell your friend something. You could phone them — but then you both have to be free at the same second, and if their phone is off you are stuck holding the message.

Or you post a letter. You drop it in the box and get on with your day. Your friend reads it when they get home. If they are on holiday for a week, the letter waits; nothing is lost and you were never blocked.

A queue is the letterbox. The trade is that you no longer know when they read it, and you cannot get an answer back in the same breath.

🎓 Professional

A synchronous HTTP call couples the caller to the callee in three ways at once: availability (if it is down, you fail), latency (your response time is the sum of theirs), and capacity (a slow consumer becomes your slow endpoint). Chain four services and your success rate is the product of four availabilities.

Putting a broker between them buys you: the producer completes without the consumer; load-levelling, because a burst becomes queue depth rather than timeouts; retries without the caller knowing; and independent scaling of each side.

What it costs is real and worth saying out loud: eventual consistency — the work is not done when you return 202 — plus a broker to operate, harder debugging across a boundary, and a whole class of new failure modes (duplicates, poison messages, ordering) that this chapter is mostly about.

Queues and topics

🧒 Simple

A queue is the line at the supermarket. There may be four cashiers, but each customer is served by exactly one of them. More cashiers, shorter line.

A topic is the school announcement on the loudspeaker. Everybody who is listening hears it — the same message, all at once.

🎓 Professional
  • Queue / point-to-point. One message, one consumer. Several instances of a consumer form a competing consumers group, and adding instances adds throughput. This is how you scale work out.
  • Topic / publish-subscribe. One message, every interested subscriber gets its own copy. This is how you decouple: publish OrderSubmitted and let three teams subscribe without ever telling you.

The naming follows the intent, and it is worth getting right in an interview: commands go to a queue and are addressed to one handler (SendInvoiceEmail — imperative, one owner). Events go to a topic and state a fact (OrderSubmitted — past tense, no expectation about who reacts). A publisher that knows what its subscribers will do has not decoupled anything.

The honest default for a .NET team: Azure Service Bus if you are already on Azure — the Azure.Messaging.ServiceBus SDK is idiomatic and there is nothing to run. RabbitMQ otherwise; it is one container and covers the vast majority of line-of-business needs. Kafka when you genuinely need a durable, replayable stream — telemetry, event sourcing, analytics pipelines. Choosing Kafka for a queue of a hundred e-mails a day is a well-known way to acquire an operations problem you did not need.

A licensing detail worth knowing (2026)

MassTransit — the abstraction most .NET teams put over these brokers — moved to a commercial licence in 2026, with v8 reaching end of life at the end of the year. This is the same thing that happened to MediatR, and it is why this repository hand-writes its dispatcher (see IDispatcher.cs). Knowing the pattern rather than the package is what turns that kind of news into a non-event. Mentioning it in an interview shows you follow the ecosystem.

The guarantee you actually get

🎓 Professional

Brokers offer at-least-once delivery. A message is redelivered if the consumer crashes before acknowledging, if the acknowledgement is lost, or if a retry fires. So duplicates are not an edge case — they are normal operation.

Exactly-once delivery does not exist in a distributed system, and claiming it in an interview is a red flag. What you can build is exactly-once processing: at-least-once delivery plus an idempotent consumer. 

// The message id is the deduplication key — this is why OutboxMessage
// carries an Id and puts it on the wire.
if (await db.ProcessedMessages.AnyAsync(m => m.Id == message.Id, ct))
    return;                                   // already handled; ack and move on

await DoTheWork(message, ct);
db.ProcessedMessages.Add(new(message.Id, DateTimeOffset.UtcNow));
await db.SaveChangesAsync(ct);              // same transaction as the work

Better still, make the operation naturally idempotent: set the status to Shipped rather than toggle it; MERGE rather than INSERT. Then a duplicate is harmless without any bookkeeping at all.

The outbox, and the bug it prevents

Here is the mistake almost every first messaging implementation makes:

the dual-write problem — there is no safe ordering
await db.SaveChangesAsync(ct);        // 1. committed
await bus.PublishAsync(orderPlaced);  // 2. process dies HERE
// → the order exists and nobody was ever told.

await bus.PublishAsync(orderPlaced);  // swap them and get the mirror bug:
await db.SaveChangesAsync(ct);        // → a message about an order that never committed.

You cannot commit to a database and a broker atomically, so no ordering of those two lines is safe. The transactional outbox makes it one write instead of two: the message is inserted as a row, in the same transaction as the state change. Either both land or neither does. A background worker then reads unprocessed rows and publishes them. 

┌─ ONE database transaction ────────────────────────┐
│  UPDATE Orders SET Status = 'Submitted' ...       │
│  INSERT INTO OutboxMessages (Id, Type, Payload)   │  ← the message is a ROW
└───────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                       │ commit: both, or neither
                       ▼
      OutboxProcessor polls, publishes, marks done

And note why this is at-least-once and not exactly-once: if the process dies after publishing but before marking the row processed, it publishes again on restart. That is fine — the consumer is idempotent. This is the shape in OutboxProcessor.cs, which is a BackgroundService and therefore a singleton that must create a DI scope per iteration rather than injecting a DbContext.

Poison messages and dead letters

⚠ The infinite redelivery loop

A message fails. The broker redelivers it. It fails again. Nothing else in the queue moves, your logs fill at thousands of lines a second, and the on-call engineer finds a 40 GB log file with one error in it repeated four million times.

The fix has three parts: a retry policy with exponential backoff and jitter (never a tight loop), a maximum delivery count, and a dead-letter queue the message lands in when that count is exceeded. The DLQ is not a bin — it is an inbox. Alert on its depth, because a message arriving there means a human must look.

⚠ Fat messages

Do not put a 12 MB PDF on the bus. Service Bus caps a standard message at 256 KB, and even where the limit is higher, large payloads destroy throughput and fill the broker's disk. Use the claim check pattern: store the blob, put the reference on the message. The same applies to putting the whole entity on the message — send the id and enough context, and let the consumer read what it needs.

⚠ Reaching for a queue where a transaction would do

If two operations must both succeed or both fail and the user is waiting for the answer, that is a database transaction, not a message. Messaging buys you decoupling in exchange for eventual consistency; spending that price where you did not need to buy anything gives you distributed-systems problems for free. "Can this be done a second later, and can it be done twice?" is the entry test.

Try it yourself
docker run -d --name rabbit -p 5672:5672 -p 15672:15672 rabbitmq:3-management
# management UI at http://localhost:15672 — guest / guest

In the UI create a queue, publish a message by hand, and watch Ready go to 1. Now the experiment: start a consumer, stop it mid-way through processing without acknowledging, and watch the message reappear as Ready. Nothing was lost, and it will be delivered again — that is at-least-once, visible in ten seconds, and it is why your handler must be idempotent. Then set a max delivery count and let one message fail repeatedly until it lands in the dead-letter queue.

Golden rules

  1. Ask first: can this happen a second later, and can it happen twice? If not, use a transaction.
  2. Commands go to a queue and have one owner. Events go to a topic and state a fact.
  3. Name events in the past tense. A publisher that knows its subscribers is still coupled.
  4. Delivery is at-least-once. Exactly-once delivery does not exist.
  5. Every consumer is idempotent, keyed on the message id — or naturally idempotent by design.
  6. Never write to the database and the broker as two separate operations. Use the outbox.
  7. Retries need exponential backoff and jitter, and a maximum delivery count.
  8. A dead-letter queue is an inbox with an alert on it, not a bin.
  9. Small messages. Store the blob, send the reference.
  10. Ordering is per queue, per session or per partition — never global. Design so it rarely matters.
  11. Carry the correlation id onto the message, or you lose the trace at the boundary.
  12. Kafka is a log, not a queue. Choose it for replay and volume, not for a hundred e-mails a day.

Interview questions

"Perché usare una coda invece di una chiamata HTTP?"

Decoupling in availability, latency and capacity: the producer completes without the consumer, a burst becomes queue depth instead of timeouts, and each side scales on its own. Then state the price honestly — eventual consistency, a broker to run, and duplicates to handle. Naming the cost is what makes the answer sound like experience.

"How do you avoid processing the same message twice?"

You do not avoid delivery twice — that is guaranteed to happen eventually. You make the consumer idempotent: deduplicate on the message id in the same transaction as the work, or design the operation so a repeat is harmless (set the status rather than toggling it). Say explicitly that exactly-once delivery is not achievable; exactly-once processing is.

"What is the outbox pattern and what problem does it solve?"

The dual-write problem: you cannot atomically commit to a database and publish to a broker, so a crash between the two either loses the message or announces something that never happened. The outbox inserts the message as a row in the same transaction, and a background worker publishes it afterwards. Then point at the code — this repository has exactly that.

"RabbitMQ or Kafka?"

Different tools. RabbitMQ is a broker that routes messages and forgets them once consumed; Kafka is a durable partitioned log where consumers hold an offset and can replay. Task queues, routing and work distribution point at RabbitMQ or Service Bus; high-volume streams, event sourcing and analytics point at Kafka. Add that on Azure the pragmatic answer is often Service Bus, because there is nothing to operate.

"A message keeps failing. What happens to it?"

It is retried with exponential backoff up to a maximum delivery count, then dead-lettered. Explain why the cap matters: without one, a poison message blocks the queue and floods the logs forever. Then the part people forget — the DLQ needs an alert and someone to read it, otherwise you have merely moved the failure somewhere quieter.
