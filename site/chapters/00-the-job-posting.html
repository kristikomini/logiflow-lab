The job posting, decoded

A job advert is not a wish list. It is a compressed description of the code the team already has. Every line implies a system that exists, a problem it caused, and a question they will ask you about it. This chapter unpacks all of them.

From the advert

All of it. This chapter is the map; the remaining twenty-one are the territory.

How to read an advert

🧒 Simple

Imagine someone puts up a sign: "Wanted: a helper who can cook pasta, wash up, and carry heavy boxes." You learn a lot from that sign. It tells you they own a kitchen. It tells you the dishwasher is probably broken. And it tells you something heavy arrives every week. The sign is not really about you — it is a photograph of their problems.

🎓 Professional

Requirements lists are written by someone who just spent six months maintaining the system. The order is rarely accidental, and the qualifiers carry weight: ottima (excellent) marks a daily activity, buona (good) marks a weekly one, and familiarità or comprensione marks something you will be taught on the job but must not be frightened of. The "nice to have" list is usually the direction the codebase is moving in — which is why it is the most useful part to prepare.

What the list implies about their codebase

Put every requirement on one picture and a specific system appears — not a generic one. This is a company running a data-heavy line-of-business application, old enough to have performance problems and new enough to be modernising.

⚠ The mistake candidates make

Preparing breadth instead of depth. Twelve technologies each known to one paragraph loses to four known well plus honest curiosity about the rest. When you cannot answer, the winning sentence is not a guess — it is "I have not used it; here is the closest thing I have used, and here is what I would go and read."

Why the chapters are in this order

The sidebar is not alphabetical and not a ranking. It follows dependency: nothing appears before the thing it needs.

  • 00a–00c, from zero. If you have never written C#: a program that runs, then variables and types, then the control flow and methods everything else is made of. Skip them only if you can already read a for loop without stopping.
  • 00d, the map. Which file you write first and what each one is allowed to know about the others. It comes before the language chapters on purpose — every chapter after it is easier once you can place the file it is about.
  • 01–08, the language. You cannot reason about EF Core before you can reason about IEnumerable, and you cannot reason about a Web API before you understand async. The last one — disposal, memory and shared state — is what everything above it is standing on.
  • 09–12, the data. SQL comes before EF Core deliberately, in the same order the advert lists them. An ORM you learn before SQL is an ORM you cannot debug.
  • 13–19, the web. REST as an idea, then the framework that implements it, then how to secure it, then the pages, then the browser — and finally the two ways the region builds real front ends.
  • 20–22, the pressure valves. Caching, search and messaging only make sense once you know what they are relieving.
  • 23–25, building it properly. Layering, testing and observability come after the parts they organise. You cannot usefully argue about where a rule belongs until you have written a few.
  • 26–30, the tools. Deliberately late: they are how the work ships, not what the work is.
  • 31–33, the region. The desktop estate, the factory and the gestionale — three markets that pay well here and that almost nobody applying has read a word about.
  • 34–38, the people. How the work is organised, how you are judged, and then the three rounds in the order you will meet them: the CV, the screening test, the interview.

Thirty-three chapters — 00a, 00b, 00c, 00d, 04, 05, 05b, 08, 08b, 08c, 14b, 15, 16b, 17b, 18, 19, 22, 22b, 23, 24, 25, 25b, 28, 29b, 31, 32, 32b, 32c, 33, 33b, 34, 36 and 37 — are not in this advert. They are here because the same skills recur across .NET adverts in Modena, Bologna, Reggio Emilia and Milano, and because interviewers ask about them whether the posting mentioned them or not. The home page lists them separately, under Beyond the advert, so the coverage claim above stays honest.

Three of them — WinForms and WPF, industrial software and Business Central — are worth a special mention. They are not on this advert and may never be on one you answer, but they are where a large share of the region's .NET money is, and they have a fraction of the competition. Read them even if you intend to ignore them; deciding not to take a road is different from not knowing it is there.

Try it yourself

Before you read another chapter, write the advert out by hand and put a mark next to each line: ✓ I could be asked this today, ~ I would survive with a hint, ✗ I would have nothing to say. Keep the sheet. Redo it after chapter 38; the difference between the two sheets is the only progress measure that matters here.

Golden rules

  1. An advert describes the employer’s existing problems, not an ideal candidate.
  2. Ottima = daily. Buona = weekly. Familiarità = do not be frightened of it.
  3. "T-SQL" listed before any ORM means real SQL is written by hand on this team.
  4. ".NET / .NET Core" with a slash means legacy and modern coexist, so migration work exists.
  5. Redis and ElasticSearch on the list are symptoms: reads repeat, and text search outgrew the database.
  6. The optional list is where candidates separate, because everyone prepares the mandatory one.
  7. "Autonomia decisionale" means tickets arrive underspecified. That is a skill, and chapter 35 treats it as one.
  8. Never bluff an unknown technology. Name the nearest thing you do know and say what you would read.

Interview questions

"Tell us about yourself."

Ninety seconds, three beats: what you build, one thing you built that matches their stack, and why this role. Not a CV read aloud. Rehearse it in Italian if the interview will be in Italian — the first answer sets the language confidence for the whole conversation.

"What do you know about our system?"

Answer with the diagram above. "From the advert I would guess a .NET Core API in front of SQL Server, some Framework applications still in production, and enough traffic that you needed Redis and a search index. Is that roughly right?" This question is a gift and most candidates waste it.

"Which of these technologies do you not know?"

Answer it honestly and immediately. The purpose of the question is calibration — they are working out whether your "yes" can be trusted. A candidate who claims all twelve is now unverifiable on all twelve.
