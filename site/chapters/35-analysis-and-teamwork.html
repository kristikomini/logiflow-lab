Analysis, autonomy and teamwork

Two lines of the advert are not technical, are not optional, and are the reason most people who fail a probation period fail it. They are also entirely learnable — they just look like personality traits, so nobody teaches them.

From the advert

"Buone capacità di analisi e autonomia decisionale nel proprio dominio di riferimento."
"Spiccato orientamento al team working e buone doti comunicative."

Translated honestly: we will give you a ticket, not a specification, and you will sit next to the people affected by what you decide.

Analysis: what to do with an underspecified ticket

🧒 Simple

Someone says: "draw me a house."

You could start drawing immediately — and produce a house with a red roof and a garden, when they wanted a block of flats. Or you could refuse to draw anything until they describe every window.

Both are wrong. What a good drawer does is ask two questions — "who lives in it?" and "is it a picture or a plan?" — then draw, and show it early.

🎓 Professional

Requirements arrive incomplete because the person writing them knows their domain, not yours. Autonomia decisionale does not mean deciding everything alone; it means knowing which decisions are yours and which are not, and not blocking on the ones that are yours.

The distinction that makes this practical is reversibility. A decision you can undo in an afternoon — a class name, a folder layout, the internal shape of a service — is yours; make it and move on. A decision that is expensive to reverse — a database schema, a public API contract, a third-party dependency, anything visible to users — deserves a conversation before you write it.

The questions that are always worth asking

QuestionWhy it saves days
"Who is going to use this, and what are they doing just before and just after?" Turns a feature into a workflow. Half of all rework is a feature that technically worked but did not fit what happened next.
"What should happen when… ?" (empty, duplicate, cancelled, expired, thirty thousand of them) Edge cases are where specifications go silent and bugs live.
"How will we know it worked?" Extracts the acceptance criteria that were never written down.
"Is there an existing screen or endpoint that already does something like this?" Consistency, and often two days of work you do not need to do.
"How urgent is this compared with what I am doing now?" Makes prioritisation someone else’s decision, which it is.
⚠ Two failure modes, equally common

Analysis paralysis — asking twenty questions and waiting. It reads as an inability to proceed, and by the third day nobody remembers the ticket.

Silent assumption — guessing and building for three days. It reads as not caring what was wanted.

The move that beats both: ask the two questions that would change your approach, state your assumptions about the rest in writing, and start. "I am assuming an order with no lines cannot be exported and we skip it silently — shout if that is wrong" is one line, unblocks you immediately, and puts the correction on record.

Deciding, and recording the decision

When a decision is yours and it is not trivially reversible, write down why. Four sentences is enough, and six months later it is the only thing standing between a colleague and re-litigating it from scratch.

# 7. Use an outbox table to feed the search index

Status: accepted · Date: 2026-09-04

## Context
Products must appear in Elasticsearch after being saved. Writing to SQL and
Elasticsearch in the same method leaves them permanently divergent whenever the
second call fails, and we have seen this happen twice.

## Decision
Write an event row in the same SQL transaction as the product, and publish it
from a background worker.

## Consequences
+ Atomic with the business data; retryable; survives an Elasticsearch outage.
− The index is eventually consistent — typically a second or two behind.
− One more moving part to monitor (the worker's queue depth).

Context, decision, consequences — including the ones you did not like. An ADR that lists only advantages is marketing, and the reader will not trust it.

Estimating without lying

🎓 Professional

An estimate is a probability distribution, not a promise, and everyone knows it. What distinguishes a useful estimate is that it names the uncertainty rather than hiding it.

  • Decompose. Anything you would estimate in weeks, break down. You cannot estimate what you have not decomposed — that is not pessimism, it is arithmetic.
  • Give a range with a reason. "Two days if the existing export service can be reused; five if it cannot. I can tell you which by tomorrow morning."
  • Time-box the unknown. A spike is a legitimate deliverable: "half a day to find out, then a real estimate."
  • Re-estimate out loud, early. The problem is never that something took longer. It is that nobody was told until the deadline.

Code review

🧒 Simple

When someone checks your homework, they are not saying you are stupid. They are the second pair of eyes that catches the thing you stopped being able to see two hours ago — because you wrote it, and now you read what you meant instead of what is there.

As the authorAs the reviewer
Keep it small. A 200-line PR gets a real review; a 2,000-line PR gets "LGTM". Review the code, never the person. "This method does two things" — not "you always do this".
Write a description saying why, and what you considered and rejected. Distinguish blocking from optional. Prefix with nit: what is preference.
Review your own diff first. Half the comments you would have received, you can make yourself. Ask rather than assert: "what happens if lines is empty here?" beats "this is broken".
Do not take it personally, and do not defend reflexively. Ask what they saw. Say what is good. A review that is only criticism trains people to fear reviews.
Reply to every comment, even if only "done" or "keeping it, because…". Be quick. A PR waiting two days blocks a person and rots against main.
Disagreeing well

Two rounds of comments, then talk — in person or on a call. A disagreement that has generated four replies each way is not going to be resolved by a fifth; it is a five-minute conversation being conducted in the worst possible medium. Then write the outcome back into the thread so the next reader knows how it ended.

Communicating while you work

The three moments that matter, and most people manage none of them:

  1. When you are blocked. The rule of thumb: try alone for thirty to sixty minutes, then ask — and ask well. "I am trying to X, I expected Y, I get Z, I have already checked A and B." That question can be answered in one line. "It does not work" costs the other person twenty minutes of interrogation.
  2. When something will be late. As soon as you know, not when it is due. Late-and-warned is a scheduling problem; late-and-silent is a trust problem.
  3. When you have finished. Say what changed, what you tested, and what you deliberately did not do. The last part prevents the assumption that it was forgotten.
🎓 Working in an Italian team

Some things worth knowing before the first day, particularly in Emilia-Romagna’s manufacturing and gestionale software world:

  • The working language is Italian; the documentation is English. Standups, reviews and hallway conversation in Italian; NuGet docs, Stack Overflow and error messages in English. Nobody expects perfect Italian from a non-native speaker, and everybody notices effort. Say "scusa, puoi ripetere?" without embarrassment — it is far better than nodding.
  • Teams are small and physically close. Five to fifteen people, often in one room, frequently with the domain expert — someone who knows the logistica or the produzione intimately — two desks away. That person is the single most valuable resource you have. Ask them things.
  • Relationships are built at lunch. The pausa pranzo is not optional social decoration; it is where half the informal coordination happens. Eating at your desk every day is read as distance, not diligence.
  • Hierarchy is present but informal. You will be on first-name terms with almost everyone; Lei is for clients and the first meeting. But do not confuse friendliness with flatness — who signs off on what is usually clear and worth learning early.
  • August is real. Much of the country slows to a halt in the middle of the month. Plan releases around it, and do not expect an answer from a supplier during Ferragosto.

Try it yourself

Here is a real ticket, in the register they are actually written in:

#812 — Export ordini

"Serve un export degli ordini in Excel per l’ufficio amministrazione."

Before writing a line of code, produce three lists:

  1. Two questions you must ask because the answer changes your approach.
  2. Four assumptions you will state and proceed on.
  3. Three edge cases the ticket does not mention.

Questions worth asking: which orders (a date range? a filter? all of them?), and who triggers it — because "the accounts office presses a button" and "it arrives by email every Monday" are different features. Assumptions: .xlsx not CSV, Italian column headers, Europe/Rome dates and 1.234,50 number formatting, only orders the user may already see. Edge cases: 200,000 rows (does it stream, or time out?); an order with no lines; concurrent exports; and cancelled orders — in or out?

Notice that number formatting and permissions appear in every such ticket and are written down in almost none of them.

Golden rules

  1. Sort decisions by reversibility. Cheap to undo: decide alone. Expensive: ask first.
  2. Ask the two questions that change your approach; state assumptions about the rest and start.
  3. Write assumptions where others can see them. An unstated assumption is a bug with a delay fuse.
  4. Edge cases are the specification. Empty, duplicate, huge, cancelled, concurrent.
  5. Estimate as a range with a reason, and time-box the unknown part.
  6. Say it will be late the day you know, not the day it is due.
  7. Small pull requests. A big one is not reviewed, it is approved.
  8. Review code, not people. Mark what is preference as preference.
  9. Two rounds of comments, then talk — and write the outcome back.
  10. Ask for help with what you tried and what you expected. Thirty to sixty minutes, then ask.
  11. Record non-obvious decisions in four sentences, consequences included.
  12. Go to lunch.

Interview questions

"Tell me about a time you disagreed with a colleague."

Pick a real technical disagreement, describe both positions fairly, say how it was resolved and what you learned. The trap is telling a story where you were obviously right and they were obviously foolish — the question is testing whether you can represent someone else’s reasoning, and that version fails it.

"What do you do when requirements are unclear?"

Describe the reversibility rule, then be concrete: two questions, written assumptions, an early slice shown to the requester before it is finished. "I ask the analyst" is a weaker answer than "I ask what would change my approach, and decide the rest myself".

"How do you handle critical code review feedback?"

Say plainly that review is about the code and that you would rather find a problem in review than in production. Then give a real example where a reviewer caught something you had missed, and what you changed as a result — specifics are what make this believable.

"You are two days from a deadline and will not make it. What do you do?"

Say so immediately, with three things: what is done, what remains, and options — ship a reduced scope, move the date, or add help (knowing help rarely speeds up a late task). Never let a deadline arrive unannounced. This question is asked because someone once did.

"How is your Italian?"

Answer honestly with a level and evidence — "B1, I follow standups and read tickets, I am slower in long meetings, I am taking a course" — and say you are happy for the technical interview to be in Italian if they prefer. Overstating gets discovered in the first five minutes; understating an honest B1 costs you nothing.
