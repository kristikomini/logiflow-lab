Traffic, OEE and commissioning

The layer above the wire: two vehicles wanting one aisle, the number on the wall and the argument underneath it, the recall query you must be able to answer in an hour — and what the job is actually like, which nobody writes down.

Chapter 32b was the protocol. This is the product those two companies actually sell — a WCS, a warehouse control system — and this repository has one: cd src/LogiFlow.Wcs && dotnet run. No PLC, no hardware, no licence.

Two vehicles, one aisle

🧒 Simple

Two people meet in a corridor exactly wide enough for one. Each has already stepped in. Each is waiting for the other to back out.

People solve this in a second, because people can improvise. Two loaded vehicles that were each told go forward through here cannot. They will still be there in the morning.

🎓 Professional

A transport order is move this load unit from A to B. To execute it a vehicle must own every zone on its route while it is in them — a stretch of aisle, a crossing, a lift — because a zone that fits one vehicle and contains two is a collision. So a traffic manager is a resource allocator: vehicles are threads, zones are locks, and the analogy holds all the way down including the part everyone hopes it will not. 

$ dotnet run traffic

── 1. Acquire in the order the route needs ──
   V1 took zone 0
   V2 took zone 1
   V1 STUCK waiting for zone 1
   V2 STUCK waiting for zone 0
   completed: 0 of 2

── 2. The same two moves, lowest zone id first ──
   V1 route 0→1, took [0, 1] — through
   V2 route 1→0, took [0, 1] — through
   completed: 2 of 2
Line by line
  • Both vehicles behaved perfectly reasonably. V1 goes north, V2 goes south, each takes the zones its route needs in the order it meets them. That is a guaranteed cycle in the wait-for graph.
  • completed: 0 of 2 — and the timeout in the demo exists only so the demo ends. On a real floor there is no timeout.
  • The fix is one line: route.Order(). A cycle needs two holders acquiring in opposite orders, so if everybody acquires ascending, opposite orders cannot happen.
  • There is a second benefit that is easy to miss. V2 now waits before it enters rather than halfway through — a vehicle blocked at the mouth of an aisle is somewhere a human can walk past; one blocked in the middle is an obstruction.
Why "add a timeout and recover" is the wrong reflex

Nothing times out on a floor. There is no supervisor process that kills a stuck vehicle and no user who gives up and refreshes.

And there is no safe recovery action. Suppose you detect the cycle — now what? Reversing a vehicle means driving backwards along a route planned forwards, past a zone that may now be occupied, possibly carrying two tonnes, in a space designed on the assumption that vehicles go one way. That is a physical manoeuvre with its own safety case, not a catch block.

So deadlock is prevented by construction, never detected and recovered from.

All-or-nothing, and the bug you introduce fixing it

Sorting the route is one correct answer. The stronger one, and what most real traffic managers do, is allocate the whole route atomically: you get all of it or none of it. A party that holds nothing until it holds everything can never be half of a deadlock, which is why the allocator in this repository has no ordering rule in it at all. 

public Allocation Request(EquipmentId vehicle, IEnumerable<string> route, DateTimeOffset nowUtc)
{
    string[] zones = [.. route.Distinct(StringComparer.OrdinalIgnoreCase)];

    // Asking while still holding is the one way back to hold-and-wait.
    if (_heldBy.Values.Contains(vehicle))
        return new Allocation(AllocationOutcome.AlreadyHolding, null);

    if (CanGrant(zones, skipQueueCheck: false))
    {
        Grant(vehicle, zones, nowUtc);
        return new Allocation(AllocationOutcome.Granted, null);
    }

    _queue.Add(new PendingRequest(vehicle, zones, nowUtc));    // FIFO, and it matters
    return new Allocation(AllocationOutcome.Queued, _queue.Count - 1);
}
Line by line
  • Real code — ZoneAllocator.cs, in the Domain layer, with no dependencies at all.
  • AlreadyHolding — refused loudly rather than tolerated. Hold-and-wait is the single route back to deadlock, so it is a caller bug, not a traffic condition.
  • skipQueueCheck: false — the anti-starvation rule. A newcomer whose route is entirely free may still not have it, if any of those zones is one a waiting vehicle is holding out for.
  • Without that, all-or-nothing trades deadlock for starvation — and starvation is worse in one specific way: deadlock is obvious and this is not. Throughput looks healthy, nobody raises a ticket, and one vehicle sits in the same spot for twenty minutes until a shift supervisor mentions it in passing.
  • It is not thread-safe, on purpose. You are granting tens of routes a second, not millions. One single-threaded component that owns the whole zone table is trivially fair, trivially auditable, and fast enough by orders of magnitude. Reaching for distributed locking here is a self-inflicted wound.
  • Zone names compare case-insensitively, because they come from a floor plan drawn by a person and AISLE-3 being a different zone from Aisle-3 is two vehicles in one aisle.

Strict FIFO would be fair and useless — one vehicle waiting for a busy aisle would stop every unrelated move in the building. So the queue is served front to back but does not stop at the first refusal: a request further down whose zones are free, and which no earlier request wants, may proceed.

Zone size is the throughput dial

   Six vehicles, four moves each, identical driving, ordered acquisition throughout.

    3 zones   fleet finished in   621 ms
   12 zones   fleet finished in   369 ms
Line by line
  • Same vehicles, same algorithm, no deadlock either way. The only thing that changed is how finely the same floor is cut into zones.
  • Coarse zones serialise moves that never actually conflict. Two vehicles at opposite ends of a long aisle are not in each other's way — but if the whole aisle is one zone, one of them waits.
  • This is what “throughput optimisation” means in a WCS advert, and it is mostly this decision rather than clever code. Worth knowing before an interview, because the instinct is to talk about algorithms and the honest answer is about modelling.
  • The dial does not go to infinity. Every boundary is an acquire and a release, so cut finely enough and vehicles spend their time asking permission. A zone smaller than a vehicle plus its braking distance is not a zone — the vehicle occupies two of them at all times and you have gained bookkeeping. And some boundaries exist because a safety scanner is there, which an engineer signed for and you model around.

OEE is a definition, not a measurement

🧒 Simple

A teacher wants one number for how good a day the class had. She multiplies three things: how much of the lesson they were actually working, how fast they worked while working, and how much of the work was any good.

Then somebody asks whether the fire drill counts as lost time. The answer changes the number, and nothing about the day changed at all.

🎓 Professional

OEE = Availability × Performance × Quality. Three fractions, multiplied. Each is a ratio of two numbers somebody had to define, and that is the whole story. dotnet run oee takes one eight-hour shift as an event log and computes it twice, changing exactly one decision: 

   planned downtime EXCLUDED
      planned production time   430 min      run time  393 min
      Availability  91.4%   Performance  94.7%   Quality  97.5%
      →  OEE  84.3%

   planned downtime INCLUDED
      planned production time   480 min      run time  393 min
      Availability  81.9%   Performance  94.7%   Quality  97.5%
      →  OEE  75.6%
Line by line
  • Same shift, same machine, same pieces. Eight and a half points. Nobody touched anything on the floor.
  • Both are defensible — they answer how well did the equipment run when we asked it to and how much of the shift did we get out of it. The standard OEE definition is the first; the second, widened to calendar time, has its own name, TEEP.
  • The plant manager sees one number and does not know which produced it. Whoever decides what counts as planned downtime moves it by several points — and that person is often you by default, because you wrote the query. Get the definition written down, agreed and dated.
  • Store the events, compute the number. You will be asked to change the definition, usually a year after the number has been on a wall. A stored percentage cannot be recomputed, explained, or restated. A table of stops with reasons and durations can be recomputed a hundred times.
The loss that hides

Micro-stops. A jam cleared by an operator in eight seconds is usually below the PLC's logging threshold, so it never becomes a stop event. The time is still gone — so it comes out of Performance instead of Availability, and the line looks slow rather than stopped. That sends improvement projects at the wrong target for a year.

If Performance is mysteriously low and nobody can explain it, look for unlogged short stops first. That is a diagnosis rather than a formula, and it is what an interviewer is hoping to hear.

And Performance above 100% is not a good day: the configured ideal cycle time is wrong, or somebody ran the line over its rated speed. Clamp it and raise a data-quality alarm. Displaying 104% quietly teaches everybody that the number is decorative.

Traceability, which is not a feature

The question is: a customer complains about this box. What was in it, and what else is affected? The answer has to name the production batch, the raw-material lots that went into it, the machine, the process values, the shift and often the operator. In food, pharma, cosmetics and automotive this is a legal obligation with named regulations behind it, and the deadline is measured in hours because the alternative is recalling everything. 

production_order      what we were asked to make
batch                 one execution of it on one line, with start and end
batch_input           batch × material lot × quantity        ← the genealogy edge
unit / pallet         what came out, with its own id
stop_event            batch × start × duration × reason × who entered it
process_sample        batch × tag × value × quality × source time   (the cheap path)
shift                 who was on, and when
Line by line
  • batch_input — this one table is the whole traceability story. Get it right and both directions of the genealogy query are a recursive CTE away. Leave it out because “the ERP already knows” and you cannot answer the question at all — which you discover during a recall, at the worst possible moment.
  • Genealogy runs both ways, and both get asked. Backwards: this box is bad, what went into it? Forwards: this raw lot was contaminated, where did it all go? The forward query decides how much product you recall, and it is the one people forget to test.
  • stop_event stores a duration and a reason, never a computed availability. Everything in the OEE section follows from that.
  • The reason is chosen by a person on a touchscreen with gloves on. Short list, their language, common ones first, never free text — or you get jam, Jam, jammed and one entry that is a phone number.
  • process_sample lives on a different path with a different lifetime: full rate for weeks, minute aggregates forever. Conflating it with the rest is how a table becomes 4 TB.
  • Append-only, retained for years. Corrections are new records with an author and a reason, never an UPDATE. Five to ten years is routine, which is a schema decision taken on day one.

Testing in simulation environments

AGV-fleet vendors ask for it by name, and it is not a nice-to-have here: there is one line, it is making product, and you may have it for four hours on a Sunday. A simulator you can run a thousand times is the only way to be confident before those four hours start. 

[Fact]
public void AnHourOfOperation_MovesWorkContinuously()
{
    (TransportDispatcher dispatcher, SimulatedPlcGateway floor, ManualClock clock) = Warehouse();

    for (int pass = 0; pass < PassesPerHour; pass++)
        RunOnePass(dispatcher, floor, clock);      // advance clock, tick floor, run the loop

    dispatcher.Completed.ShouldBeGreaterThan(100);

    int secondHalf = dispatcher.Completed - completedAtHalfway;
    secondHalf.ShouldBeGreaterThan(completedAtHalfway / 2);
}
Line by line
  • An hour of warehouse operation, in under a second, deterministically. The clock is a test double the test advances by hand and the simulator is seeded, so a failure is reproducible rather than a story about a bad afternoon.
  • The second assertion is the real one. What is being checked is not a number — it is that the warehouse keeps working. A system that deadlocks, leaks a zone or starves a vehicle produces a healthy first few minutes and then a flat line, which a short test would happily pass.
  • Nothing sleeps. A throughput assertion that depended on real delays would be a flaky test dressed up as a guarantee.
  • A simulator earns its place by being wrong in a known way. This one models travel, faults latching, commands being refused and a 40 ms scan lag — and its own documentation lists what it does not model: no packet loss or reordering, no electrical noise, no clock drift. That list is worth more than the simulator.
The restart problem, which a database does not solve

After a crash the vehicles are physically where they are, holding real space, whatever your process believes. An allocator that comes back from a table it wrote before it died will route a second vehicle into an occupied aisle while the software is convinced everything is fine.

You rebuild the zone table from where the machines report themselves to be, never from your own last write. That is the failure mode with a safety dimension rather than an availability one, and it is a very good thing to be able to say out loud.

What the job is actually like

Nobody writes this down, and the single biggest reason people leave these jobs is discovering the lifestyle after signing.

Three questions to ask them, in the first interview
  1. “How much trasferta, realistically, in weeks per year?” The honest answer ranges from four days to twenty weeks. Ask for the number.
  2. “Who owns the PLC side, and how do we work together?” This tells you whether there is a real automation team or whether “the software guy” absorbs everything — and whether a mystery takes an hour or a week.
  3. “What happens at 03:00 when a line is down?” Is there a rota, is it paid, how often does it fire, can you connect remotely. A company with a good answer has thought about its people.
And the sentence that makes the rest credible

“I am a .NET developer, not a PLC programmer, and I would not claim otherwise. I work above the PLC — I take the tags, keep the record, dispatch the work and answer to the ERP. To understand what I am being handed I wrote a Modbus client and server by hand and an OPC UA client.”

That is respected. They are not looking for a second electrical engineer; they are looking for somebody who can write maintainable C# and talk to the electrical engineer without either of them getting frustrated. Claiming ladder logic you have not written is discovered in one follow-up question.

Golden rules

  1. Nothing times out on a floor. Deadlock is prevented by construction, never detected and recovered.
  2. A vehicle that never holds part of a route can never be half of a deadlock.
  3. All-or-nothing plus retry trades deadlock for starvation, which is worse because the dashboard looks healthy. Queue FIFO.
  4. Zone size is the throughput dial, and tuning it beats clever code.
  5. One single-threaded allocator beats distributed locks. You are granting tens per second, not millions.
  6. Journal every grant and release. Why did vehicle 7 wait four minutes is a question you will be asked.
  7. Rebuild zone state from where the vehicles say they are, never from a table written before the crash.
  8. Store the events and compute the number. A stored OEE cannot be explained or restated.
  9. Whoever defines planned downtime sets the OEE.
  10. Performance above 100% is a data-quality alarm, not a good day.
  11. Unexplained low Performance means unlogged micro-stops until proven otherwise.
  12. Traceability is a legal obligation with an hours-long deadline, and the forward query is the one that decides how much you recall.
  13. Commissioning is the job, not the end of it.

Interview questions

"Two AGVs are stopped facing each other in an aisle. What went wrong, and how do you prevent it?"

Each holds a zone the other needs, so there is a cycle in the wait-for graph. It cannot be recovered by timeout, because nothing on a floor times out — they will still be there tomorrow — and reversing a loaded vehicle is a physical manoeuvre with a safety case, not an exception handler. Prevent it by construction: a total order on zone ids so everybody acquires ascending, or an allocator that grants a whole route atomically. If you choose atomic grants, add FIFO queueing, or you have traded deadlock for starvation.

"How would you make the warehouse move more pallets per hour?"

Look at zone granularity before looking at code. Coarse zones serialise moves that never actually conflict, and re-cutting the floor plan is usually worth more than any algorithmic change. Then measure the maximum wait per transport order rather than the mean, because a healthy average hides one vehicle starving. The limits on the other side are real too: every boundary costs an acquire and a release, a zone smaller than a vehicle is bookkeeping, and safety boundaries are not yours to move.

"The ERP says 500 units were produced; the MES says 496. Who is right?"

Neither, until you know what each is counting. The usual causes are a boundary — pieces made after the ERP's cut-off, or scrap counted at a different station — and a definition, such as whether a reworked piece counts once or twice. The answer is not to make the numbers agree; it is to show from the event log exactly which four pieces differ and why. Which is only possible if you stored events rather than a total.

"Your OEE went from 84% to 76% overnight and nothing changed on the line. What happened?"

Somebody changed what counts as planned downtime — almost certainly in the query, not on the floor. Excluding planned stops from the denominator answers "how well did the equipment run when we asked it to"; including them answers "how much of the shift did we get". Both are defensible and the difference here is eight and a half points. This is why the definition has to be written down and dated, and why you store the stop events rather than the percentage.

"How would you test a warehouse control system before it is installed?"

Against a simulator, driven by an injected clock so a simulated hour runs in milliseconds and deterministically. Assert that work keeps moving over a long run rather than that a number is hit once — a system that deadlocks or leaks a zone looks fine for the first few minutes. Assert the invariants directly: never two vehicles in one zone, never more zones held than vehicles can hold. And write down what the simulator does not model, because that list is the set of bugs it will never catch.

"How much travel is in this role?"

Asking this is the point. Cantiere and trasferta in the advert mean a meaningful part of the job happens at the customer's plant until they sign — and the honest range across companies is four days to twenty weeks a year. Ask for weeks, whether it is Italy or abroad, how long a typical trip is, and how weekends are handled. An experienced candidate asks; it is not an awkward question, it is a signal.
