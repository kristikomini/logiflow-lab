The edge, and reading the advert

Below the PLC there is one more layer: a byte stream on a wire, a bus with no addresses, and a box with no operating system. Some of that is your job and most of it is not — and the word embedded appears in two completely different adverts in this province. This chapter is the last mile of the machine side, and the filter that stops you answering the wrong advert.

From the adverts

One Modena advert wants “sviluppatore C#/.NET per software di supervisione e interfacciamento macchine” with protocolli seriali and Modbus RTU. Another, three rows down the same search, wants “sviluppatore firmware embedded” in C/C++ su STM32 with FreeRTOS and debug JTAG. Both say embedded. Only one of them is a job you can do, and the difference is worth learning in thirty seconds rather than in a rejected application.

Both demos here run on your laptop with no hardware and no licence: dotnet run serial and dotnet run can.

Two adverts, one word

🧒 Simple

Two shops both advertise for someone who “works with cars”.

One wants a person to drive the van, plan the routes and keep the delivery book. The other wants a person to rebuild the gearbox. Both adverts are honest, both use the same three words, and if you turn up at the second one holding a delivery book, everybody's morning is wasted.

The trick is not being better at either job. It is reading which shop you are standing in.

🎓 Professional

Search embedded in this province and you get two populations. They read alike in the first paragraph and separate completely by the third. Scan for this vocabulary before you read anything else — three or more from the right-hand column and you can stop.

Advert C: the Linux edge gateway. .NET on ARM64 on a fanless box, MQTT northbound, Modbus southbound, Docker, no Start menu. That one is yours — it is ordinary C# — and it is less contested than advert A precisely because a lot of .NET developers will not touch a machine they cannot remote-desktop into.

A byte stream has no messages

🧒 Simple

Someone is reading you a shopping list through a letterbox, one letter at a time, and they never pause between items.

If you write down whatever you hear each time you happen to be listening, you get “milkbrea” and then “deggs”. Nothing was lost on the way — you just cut it in the wrong places, because the pauses you were relying on were never part of what they were saying.

🎓 Professional

SerialPort.Read makes exactly one promise: some bytes that have arrived. Not your message, not one message, not a whole message. The protocol has framing; the stream does not. This is the most common bug in device integration and it survives every test written at a desk, because at a desk there is one device and it is slow.

Run the demo and watch it happen:

$ dotnet run serial

The read loop everybody writes first — one Read, one message:

    int n = stream.Read(buffer);
    Handle(Encoding.ASCII.GetString(buffer, 0, n));   // <- the bug

  first Read returned 51 bytes:
    <STX>W,12.480,kg,ST<ETX>a<STX>W,12.481,kg,ST<ETX>`<STX>W,12.479,kg,ST<ETX>g

  frame 1  [ok ]  W,12.480,kg,ST
  frame 2  [ok ]  W,12.481,kg,ST
  frame 3  [ok ]  W,12.479,kg,ST
  frame 4  [ok ]  W,00.000,kg,US

  5 bytes left in the buffer: <STX>W,12
Line by line
  • One Read returned three weighings. The scale had a transmit buffer and it flushed it. A reader that treats a read as a message logs 12.480 and silently loses two — no exception, no log line, just a gap nobody notices until an audit.
  • <STX> and <ETX> — bytes 0x02 and 0x03, the framing this device chose. Almost every serial device has some equivalent: a start byte, a length prefix, or a newline.
  • The single character after each <ETX> is the checksum — an LRC, the XOR of the body. It is weak on purpose: it is not cryptography, it is there because a cable running past a frequency converter occasionally delivers a byte nobody sent.
  • Frame 4 arrived in two pieces 120 ms apart and still decodes, because the reader keeps its buffer across reads instead of starting fresh each time.
  • 5 bytes left in the buffer — not a leak. It is the start of a frame the scale has not finished sending. Carrying that remainder to the next read is the technique; throwing it away is the bug wearing a different hat.

The read loop that is correct, in four steps

  1. Append whatever arrived to a buffer that lives across reads.
  2. Scan that buffer for a complete frame, by this device's rule.
  3. Take complete frames out and leave the remainder alone.
  4. Discard anything before the first start marker. On a line that was already running when you opened the port, the first thing you read is the tail of a frame that started before you existed.

Note what is not on that list: a bigger buffer, and a Thread.Sleep before reading. Both are the fixes people reach for first, both appear to work, and both are timing assumptions that the customer's site will falsify.

RS-232, RS-485, and the bug that waits for the customer

The advert says seriale. That word covers two incompatible things, and the difference is not a detail of wiring — it changes the shape of your code. 

RS-232   point to point, full duplex
         one device, one port, both directions at once  →  everything above works

RS-485   a party line: up to 32 devices on ONE pair of wires, HALF duplex
         exactly one may transmit at a time, and nothing enforces that

      master  ──ask #1──▶
                         ◀──answer──      slave 1 needs 3.5 character times
      master  ──ask #2──▶                 to turn the line around
              ▲
              └─ sent too early: the driver is still enabled, two devices are
                 driving the same wire, and both messages become noise
Line by line
  • Half duplex is the whole story. RS-485 is a polling protocol by necessity, not by taste — which is exactly why Modbus RTU looks the way it does, with one master asking and everyone else silent.
  • The silence is part of the protocol. After a request the master must wait for the slave to stop driving the line. Conventionally 3.5 character times; at 9600 baud that is around 3.6 ms, which feels like an eternity and is not optional.
  • This is why it works here and fails at the customer. At your desk: one device, huge gaps, no collision. On site: four devices, a longer cable, a tighter poll loop. The symptom is intermittent checksum errors, not a crash — a timing bug in code that reads correctly.
  • One port, one owner, one thread. Two threads sharing an RS-485 port is not fixable with a lock around Write, because the unit of mutual exclusion is the request and its answer, not the call.
  • Termination and biasing are real, and not your department. When the electrician on site says the bus needs terminating resistors, recognising the sentence saves you an afternoon spent reading your own code.

CAN, where the number is the priority

🧒 Simple

A room where everybody may speak at once, and nobody is in charge.

The rule is that everyone says their badge number first, out loud, together — and the moment you hear someone saying a lower number than yours, you stop talking and listen. One person is always left speaking, nobody was interrupted mid-sentence, and no time was spent deciding whose turn it was.

Nobody says who they are talking to, either. Everybody hears everything.

🎓 Professional

CAN has no addresses. Nothing in a frame says who sent it or who it is for. The 11-bit identifier names the message — “engine data”, “wheel speeds” — and every node hears every frame and decides for itself whether it cares. You do not request a value; you listen for it.

Around Modena this is Motor Valley vocabulary: test benches, vehicle data logging, agricultural and construction machinery. In a .NET advert it appears as acquisizione dati CAN, normally behind a vendor USB-CAN adapter with a C# API. 

$ dotnet run can

    0x18F   00110001111
    0x0C0   00011000000
    0x123   00100100011

    bit 10: all agree, continue
    bit  9: all agree, continue
    bit  8: 0x18F, 0x123 send 1, read 0 → back off
    bit  7: one node left — 0x0C0 has the bus

One frame, id 0x0C0, payload 1A 2B 64 00 00 00 00 00

  EngineSpeed  bits  0..15
                Intel    (little-endian):     2758.5 rpm
                Motorola (big-endian):          5685 rpm
Line by line
  • Arbitration is electrical, not scheduled. Each node watches the wire while it writes. A node that sends a 1 and reads a 0 has lost and stops immediately — and the winner's frame was never corrupted, so nothing is retransmitted and no time is lost.
  • The lower identifier always wins. That is not a convention layered on top; it falls out of the arbitration. It is why engine control sits at 0x0C0 and the door mirrors at 0x6xx.
  • The consequence to say out loud in an interview: a low-priority frame has no guaranteed delivery time on a loaded bus. When a CAN advert says real-time, it means a worst case somebody calculated, not a fast average somebody measured.
  • Signals are packed at bit offsets, rarely byte-aligned — a 12-bit value starting at bit 20 is ordinary — with a scale and an offset applied afterwards. The meaning lives outside the protocol, in a .dbc file somebody emails you.
  • 2758 rpm and 5685 rpm are the same eight bytes. There are two bit-order conventions, Intel and Motorola, and the frame does not say which. Nothing throws; both are believable engine speeds. You find out you chose wrong when a value crosses a threshold in the wrong place, weeks later.
You have met this bug already

This is chapter 32b's 32-bit Modbus counter, exactly: a protocol that fixes the order inside a unit and says nothing about the order between units, documentation that is a file rather than a specification, and a failure mode that is a plausible number instead of an exception. Learn it once and you have it for both.

And the sentence that matters most: Windows is not a real-time operating system, and neither is Linux by default. You can meet a 100 ms deadline reliably. You cannot promise 1 ms, because a garbage collection, a page fault or a driver can take the CPU away from you at any moment. Hard timing belongs in the PLC or the microcontroller, which is precisely why those things exist.

That is not a weakness to hide. It is the correct architectural answer, and offering it unprompted marks you as somebody who has thought about the boundary rather than somebody who has not noticed there is one.

Golden rules

  1. A read is not a message. Read returns whatever arrived — buffer across reads, scan for frames, keep the remainder.
  2. Frame explicitly. Start byte, end byte, length prefix or delimiter: the device chose one, and your reader implements it rather than assuming timing.
  3. Discard bytes before the first start marker. You joined a conversation already in progress.
  4. Verify the checksum, count the failures, and never treat one as fatal. The rate is the diagnostic, not the event.
  5. A bigger buffer and a Sleep before reading are not fixes. They are timing assumptions that hold at your desk and fail on site.
  6. RS-232 is point to point; RS-485 is a party line. Seriale in an advert does not say which, and the difference is the whole design.
  7. On RS-485 the silence is part of the protocol. Wait for the turnaround, or collide with the answer to your last question.
  8. One port, one owner. The unit of mutual exclusion is the request and its answer, not the call to Write.
  9. A CAN frame has no addresses. The identifier names the message, and everyone hears everything.
  10. On CAN the lower identifier always wins, so a low-priority frame has no guaranteed delivery time on a loaded bus.
  11. Bit order is not in the frame. Intel or Motorola lives in the .dbc, and choosing wrong yields a plausible number rather than an error.
  12. Windows is not an RTOS. Hard deadlines belong below you, and saying so is the correct answer rather than an admission.
  13. Classify the advert before you apply. Embedded beside C#, Modbus and supervisione is your job; embedded beside C, FreeRTOS and STM32 is somebody else's.
  14. Never claim firmware, for the same reason you never claim PLC programming: it is discovered in one follow-up question, and it costs you everything else you said.

Interview questions

"You open a serial port and read. What do you get?"

Whatever has arrived — which may be half a message, three messages, or nothing. The protocol has framing and the stream does not, so I accumulate into a buffer that lives across reads, scan it for complete frames, take those out and leave the remainder. The bug I am avoiding is the one-read-one-message loop, which passes at a desk with one slow device and loses data the moment the device gets busy.

"The integration works in the office and fails at the customer. Where do you look first?"

If it is RS-485, at timing and topology before code. On site there are more devices on the bus, a longer cable and usually a faster poll loop, so the first suspects are the turnaround delay before the next request, two owners of one port, and termination. The tell is intermittent checksum errors rather than a crash — a code bug is reproducible and this one is not.

"What is the difference between RS-232 and RS-485?"

232 is point to point and full duplex; 485 is a multi-drop bus, half duplex, up to 32 devices on one pair of wires. The consequence is that 485 has to be polled with exactly one talker at a time and enforced silence between, which is why Modbus RTU is shaped the way it is.

"How does CAN decide who transmits when two nodes start at once?"

Bit-by-bit arbitration on the identifier. Each node reads the wire while it writes; a node that sends a recessive bit and reads a dominant one has lost and stops immediately. The lowest identifier wins, its frame is not corrupted, and nothing is retransmitted — which is why the identifier is the priority, and why safety-critical messages are given low numbers.

"The same eight bytes of CAN payload give two different engine speeds. How?"

Bit order. Signals are packed at bit offsets and there are two conventions, Intel and Motorola; nothing in the frame says which, so the .dbc file is the only source of truth. It is the same class of problem as a 32-bit counter split across two Modbus registers, and it has the same nasty property — the wrong answer is a plausible number, not an exception.

"Can you guarantee a 1 ms response in C# on Windows?"

No, and I would not design something that needed me to. A GC pause, a page fault or a driver can take the CPU at any time, so I can be reliable at tens of milliseconds and not below. Hard timing belongs in the PLC or the drive. What I own is everything above that — the tags, the record, the dispatch and the answer to the ERP — designed so that my worst case does not stop the line.

"Have you written firmware?"

No. I write the software that talks to it — serial framing, Modbus, CAN decoding, OPC UA — and I have implemented those by hand rather than only through a library, so I know what I am being handed. Firmware on a microcontroller in C is a different job and I would not claim it. That boundary is what makes the rest of what I claim worth believing.
