Industrial software and MES

Everything so far assumed your software serves people. This chapter is about software that serves machines — and it is where a very large share of the .NET work in Emilia-Romagna actually is. The technology is familiar (C#, SQL Server, WPF). The assumptions are not.

Why this chapter exists

Search the job boards for the towns rather than the technologies. Sassuolo, Fiorano Modenese, Castel Bolognese, Ravenna, Bologna: "sviluppatore .NET/SQL per l'automazione industriale", "software gestionali e supervisioni", "esperienza in ambienti produttivi". This is the ceramics district, the packaging valley and the motor valley buying software. Almost nobody applying for these roles has read anything about the domain, which makes one chapter's worth of vocabulary disproportionately valuable.

The pyramid everyone in the room already knows

🧒 Simple

Think of a big kitchen in a restaurant.

At the bottom there is the oven, which just gets hot. Above it, a little dial that keeps the oven at exactly 200°. Above that, the head chef who says which dish is being cooked now, writes down how many came out burnt, and notices the oven was off for twenty minutes. And at the top, the manager who takes the orders from customers and decides what the kitchen will cook this week.

Everyone has a level. The manager never touches the dial, and the dial has no idea what a customer is. MES is the head chef.

🎓 Professional

The industry organises this as ISA-95, and it is the shared vocabulary in any meeting about factory software. Five levels:

What an MES is actually asked to do

  • Dispatch. Take the ERP's production order and tell this machine to make this quantity of this product, now.
  • Capture. Count good pieces and scrap, record the cause of each stoppage, log process values that a certification requires.
  • OEE. Overall Equipment Effectiveness = Availability × Performance × Quality. One number the plant manager looks at every morning; getting it right is most of the reporting work.
  • Traceability. Given a customer complaint about a box, say which batch it came from, which raw material lot went into it, which machine ran it, at what temperature, and who was on shift. In food, pharma and automotive this is a legal obligation, not a feature.
  • Instructions and quality. Show the operator the right recipe and the right checks, and refuse to proceed when a check fails.

How the data gets in

🎓 Professional
  • OPC UA — the standard, and the one to name. Platform-independent, secure by design (certificates, encrypted sessions), and it carries an information model, not just numbers: a tag knows it is a temperature in °C belonging to a machine. You subscribe to tags and receive changes. The OPCFoundation.NetStandard.Opc.Ua packages are the usual .NET client.
  • MQTT — lightweight publish/subscribe, common with newer IIoT sensors and for pushing data out to the cloud. Often paired with Sparkplug B for structure.
  • Direct PLC drivers — Siemens S7, Modbus TCP, EtherNet/IP. Older, cheaper installations still work this way and you will meet raw register maps where the documentation is a spreadsheet.
  • Humans and barcodes. Never forget this one. A scanner is a keyboard, an operator terminal is a WinForms or WPF screen with big buttons for gloved hands, and the stoppage reason is chosen from a list by a person.
  • Files. A CSV dropped on a share every ten minutes by a machine from 2004. It is not elegant and it will outlive you.

Why this software is written differently

🧒 Simple

A website going down is annoying: people come back later. A factory line going down costs thousands of euro an hour and a hundred people stand still.

So the rule is different. On a website you ask "is this the nicest way to build it?" In a factory you ask "what happens if this breaks at three in the morning and nobody is here?"

🎓 Professional

Five assumptions from web work that are simply false here:

  1. "The database is always reachable." It is not, and the line cannot stop because your server is patching. Acquisition buffers locally and forwards when the link returns — store and forward. This is the single most important design idea in the chapter.
  2. "A day starts at midnight." It starts when the shift starts. A shift runs 22:00–06:00 and belongs to one production day; the night of the clock change has 23 or 25 hours; the plant may run a calendar with Ferragosto shutdown in it. Time is a business concept, not a timestamp.
  3. "A few thousand rows a day." One machine sampling ten tags at 5 Hz is four million rows a day. Multiply by forty machines. You will be doing partitioning, aggregation into shift-level roll-ups, and a retention policy from day one — the material in chapter 11, but with the volume knob turned up.
  4. "We can deploy at any time." You deploy during the shutdown, and the shutdown may be in August. Changes are validated; in pharma and food they are qualified, with documentation.
  5. "Everything is on the internet." The OT network is deliberately isolated from IT, often through a DMZ. There may be no outbound internet on the shop floor at all, which decides your deployment story, your NuGet story and your telemetry story.

A data model you can sketch in an interview

Machine(Id, Code, LineId, ...)
Shift(Id, PlantId, StartsAtLocal, EndsAtLocal, ProductionDate)   -- NOT derived from the clock
WorkOrder(Id, ErpOrderNo, ProductId, QuantityPlanned, MachineId, StartedAtUtc, EndedAtUtc)
ProductionEvent(Id, MachineId, WorkOrderId, OccurredAtUtc, Good, Scrap)
Downtime(Id, MachineId, FromUtc, ToUtc, ReasonId, OperatorId)    -- reason: planned? failure? changeover?
Reading(MachineId, TagId, OccurredAtUtc, Value)                  -- the big one; partition by date
Lot(Id, WorkOrderId, MaterialLotId)                              -- what went into what

Two decisions in there are the whole conversation. ProductionDate is stored, not computed — the moment you calculate it from a timestamp you have signed up for every shift, midnight and DST bug there is. And every downtime has a reason chosen by a human, because "the machine stopped" is data and "the machine stopped because the film reel ran out" is information, and only the second one improves OEE.

⚠ The three that will actually bite you
  • Machine clocks lie. A PLC's clock drifts, or was set by hand in 2019, or has no time zone. Timestamp on arrival and keep the machine's own value, and reconcile. Never assume they agree.
  • Local time in the database. Store UTC, plus the plant's time zone, plus the production date. Storing local time means an hour of your data is ambiguous every October and missing every March.
  • Blocking the acquisition loop. The OPC UA subscription callback is not the place to write to SQL Server. One slow query and you drop samples that no longer exist anywhere. Enqueue and hand off — the acquisition thread does nothing but acquire.
Try it yourself

You do not need a factory. Microsoft publishes an OPC UA server that simulates a PLC with moving tags: 

docker run -d --rm -p 50000:50000 --name opcplc \
  mcr.microsoft.com/iotedge/opc-plc:latest \
  --pn=50000 --autoaccept --sph --sn=5 --sr=10 --st=uint

# then, in a console app:
dotnet add package OPCFoundation.NetStandard.Opc.Ua.Client

Connect, browse the address space, and subscribe to a simulated tag. Then do the exercise that is really the point: write the samples through a bounded in-memory queue into SQLite, and pull the network cable (stop the SQL container). The collector must keep buffering and the tag must keep arriving. Reconnect, and everything catches up with no gap and no duplicates. Build that once and you can talk about industrial software honestly.

Golden rules

  1. Learn the ISA-95 levels. It is the vocabulary of every meeting you will be in.
  2. OPC UA between Level 2 and 3; B2MML or a REST/ERP integration between 3 and 4.
  3. The line never stops for your software. Buffer locally, forward later.
  4. Store the production date; never derive it from a timestamp.
  5. UTC in the database, plus the plant's time zone. Local time is a future outage.
  6. The machine clock and your clock disagree. Record both.
  7. Never do I/O inside the acquisition callback. Enqueue and hand off.
  8. A stoppage without a reason code is not worth recording.
  9. OEE = Availability × Performance × Quality. Know what each factor excludes.
  10. Plan retention and aggregation before the first row. Raw tag data grows faster than you think.
  11. Assume no internet on the OT network, and design deployment for that.
  12. Traceability is a legal obligation in food, pharma and automotive. A gap is a finding.

Interview questions

"Ha mai lavorato in ambito produttivo?"

If the honest answer is no, the good version is: "Not in production, but I understand the shape — Level 3 between SCADA and ERP, data in over OPC UA, and the constraint that the line cannot wait for my software. I have built a collector against a simulated OPC UA server that buffers locally and forwards." That is a far better answer than pretending, and it is true after one evening.

"What is the difference between SCADA, MES and ERP?"

Time horizon and question. SCADA is now — tags, alarms, operator screens. MES is the shift and the order — dispatch, production and quality capture, OEE, traceability. ERP is weeks — orders, stock, cost. Then add the failure consequence for each, because that is what shows you understand rather than memorised.

"A shift runs 22:00 to 06:00. Which day does it belong to?"

Whichever day the plant's calendar says, which is why the production date is a stored column and not a computed one. Then raise the follow-ups yourself: the clock-change night has 23 or 25 hours, and a shift can be extended by an hour of overtime. Someone who has been near a factory will smile at this answer.

"The database is unavailable for an hour. What happens to the production data?"

Nothing is lost, because acquisition writes to a local buffer and forwards when the link returns, at-least-once, deduplicated on arrival. Say why it matters in this domain specifically: a gap in the record is a traceability finding, and the line will not be paused for your outage.

"How would you calculate OEE?"

Availability (run time ÷ planned production time) × Performance (actual output ÷ theoretical output at ideal rate) × Quality (good pieces ÷ total pieces). Then the sentence that shows real understanding: the arguments are always about what counts as planned production time — is a changeover downtime or not? — so the definition has to be agreed with the plant, and written down, before you code it.
