Incidents, and the documents you write

Chapter 25 gave you the instruments: structured logs, correlation ids, health checks. This one is the procedure — what you do in the forty minutes after somebody says "the orders page is broken", who decides how serious it is, and what you write down afterwards so it does not happen twice.

From the advert

One advert lists five responsibilities and three of them are this chapter: "analisi e documentazione", "gestire i processi di incident management e bug fixing", and "garantire il monitoraggio costante delle applicazioni in esercizio".

Note what that describes: a role that keeps existing software alive, not one that starts new projects. That is the honest shape of most junior .NET work in this region, and it is a genuinely good place to learn — production is where you find out which of your beliefs about the system were true.

The shape of an incident

🧒 Simple

There is water coming through the kitchen ceiling.

You do not begin by working out which pipe failed and why the builder chose that pipe in 2003. You turn the water off. Then you put a bucket down. Only then, with the kitchen no longer being destroyed, do you go and find the pipe.

Everybody knows this about plumbing and forgets it about software, because finding the bug is the interesting part.

🎓 Mitigate first, and know your three levers
  1. Roll back. If a deploy went out in the last hour, this is almost always the answer, and you do not need to know which change caused it. A rollback you can perform in two minutes is worth more than any amount of cleverness — which is the real argument for the small, frequent releases of chapter 30.
  2. Turn it off. A feature flag, a configuration switch, a disabled scheduled job. Losing one feature beats losing the application.
  3. Take the load off. Scale out, disable the expensive report, shed the batch job that runs at nine.

Then say so out loud in the channel: "rolled back to 4.12, orders page is responding again, still investigating". Half of incident management is other people knowing what is true right now.

Severity, decided at 09:00 on a Monday

Every team has severity levels and no team has a rulebook that resolves the actual cases. The usable question is not "how broken is it" but "who is unable to do what, and is there a way round".

What it meansResponse
Sev 1 Everyone is stopped, or money or data is being lost right now Drop everything, tell people, mitigate. Nobody is annoyed at being woken for a real Sev 1.
Sev 2 An important function is broken; there is a painful workaround Today. Not tonight.
Sev 3 Broken but avoidable; a subset of users, a cosmetic fault The backlog, with a ticket that will still make sense in a month.
⚠ Two failure modes, and the second is worse

Under-calling is the obvious risk: sitting on something serious because you are not sure and do not want to look dramatic. The rule that fixes it — and the one to say in an interview — is that escalating and being wrong costs a colleague ten minutes; not escalating and being wrong costs the company a day. Escalate.

Over-calling is the one nobody warns you about. A team where everything is Sev 1 stops responding to Sev 1. Data loss is not the same as a slow page, and treating them alike is how the alarm becomes background noise.

Alerting worth having

Chapter 25 built health checks. This is the question of what should wake somebody, and there is a single principle: alert on symptoms, not on causes.

✗ Cause alerts✓ Symptom alerts
CPU above 85%The p99 of POST /orders is above 2s for 5 minutes
Memory above 90%The 5xx rate is above 1% for 5 minutes
A queue has items in itThe oldest message in the queue is older than 10 minutes
A service restartedThe outbox has undispatched rows older than 5 minutes

A cause alert fires when nothing is wrong — high CPU during a nightly batch is healthy — and stays quiet when something is. A symptom alert says a user is having a bad time, which is the only reason to wake anybody. The queue examples are the pattern generalised: measure the age of the oldest unprocessed thing, not the count, because a big queue that is draining is fine and a small one that is stuck is not.

🎓 Alert fatigue is a real failure, with a real fix

An alert nobody acts on is worse than no alert, because it trains the team to ignore the channel and it hides the one that matters. The discipline: every alert must have an action. If the only response is "yeah, that happens", either fix the underlying issue, change the threshold, or delete the alert — deliberately, and say you did.

A runbook is what makes that concrete: a short page per alert saying what it means, how to confirm it, and the first two things to try. Writing one is an excellent first contribution from a junior, because you have to understand the system to write it and everybody benefits when you do.

Reading production: the drill

You cannot attach a debugger. Chapter 27's skills do not apply here; this is a different one, and it is a sequence you should be able to run without thinking.

from a complaint to a cause, in five moves
// 1. Get an identifier. Not "it's broken" — a correlation id from the error
//    screen, or an order number, or a timestamp plus a username.
//    This single question saves more time than any tool.

// 2. Find the request.
   traces | where customDimensions.CorrelationId == "9f2c…"

// 3. Read the whole request's story, in order. Structured logging is what
//    makes this possible — this is the payoff chapter 25 promised.
   | order by timestamp asc
   | project timestamp, message, severityLevel, customDimensions

// 4. Widen: is this one user or everyone? The answer changes the severity
//    and usually the cause.
   requests | where timestamp > ago(1h) | summarize count() by resultCode, bin(timestamp, 1m)

// 5. Correlate with change. What deployed, what configuration changed, what
//    did the third party do? Most incidents are caused by a change, and most
//    of those changes were ours.
⚠ "It works on my machine"

When it works locally and fails in production, it is nearly always one of four differences, and running through them beats staring at the code:

  1. Configuration — a setting that is absent rather than wrong, silently binding to a default (chapter 16b).
  2. Culture and time zone — your machine is it-IT, the container is invariant and UTC. Chapter 23.
  3. Data — production has the customer with 400,000 orders, the null surname, the emoji in the address field.
  4. Concurrency — you were the only user. Chapter 08 and chapter 11.

The write-up, and why it is blameless

🧒 Simple

If the rule is "whoever breaks it gets in trouble", then the fastest way to stay out of trouble is to not mention it. So people stop mentioning things, and you find out much later, when it is much worse.

Blameless is not kindness. It is the only arrangement in which you find out what actually happened.

the whole document — half a page is plenty
# Incident 2026-03-04 — orders page returning 500 for 38 minutes

**Impact.**    ~1,200 users, 14:02–14:40 UTC. 61 orders failed at checkout;
               all retried successfully afterwards. No data loss.

**Detection.** Symptom alert on the 5xx rate, 14:05. (3 minutes.)

**Mitigation.** Rolled back to 4.12 at 14:31. Page recovered at 14:40.

**Cause.**     Release 4.13 added a required configuration key. It was set in
               staging and not in production. Binding left it null; the null
               reached the carrier client and threw on every request.

**Timeline.**  14:02 deploy · 14:05 alert · 14:11 acknowledged ·
               14:22 correlated with the deploy · 14:31 rollback · 14:40 clear.

## Five whys
1. The page 500'd — the carrier client threw on a null base address.
2. Why null? — the configuration key was missing in production.
3. Why missing? — it was added to the staging file only.
4. Why did that ship? — nothing checks that required settings exist.
5. Why not? — options are bound but never validated at start-up.
   ↑ THIS is the cause worth fixing. Everything above it is a symptom.

## Actions
- [ ] ValidateOnStart on every Options class          (owner, date)
- [ ] Fail the pipeline when staging and production keys diverge
- [ ] Runbook entry: "5xx after a deploy → roll back first"
🎓 Read where the five whys stopped

It stopped at "options are bound but never validated at start-up" — a property of the system. It did not stop at "Marco forgot to add the key", which is where an unhealthy postmortem stops, and which produces the action item "be more careful". Nobody has ever been more careful.

The test for a good write-up: every action item is a change to code, configuration or process — something that would prevent this class of incident even with the same people making the same mistake. In this case the fix would have turned a 38-minute outage into a deployment that refused to start.

The documents you actually write

"Redigere analisi tecniche e documentazione funzionale" is the vaguest bullet in any advert and it means four concrete artefacts. Chapter 35 covers reading an underspecified ticket; these are what comes out the other end.

DocumentAnswersLength
Technical analysis What the business asked for, what that means in this system, which components change, what could go wrong, and roughly how long One page. Written before the code.
ADR (architecture decision record) Context, the decision, the alternatives rejected, and the consequences accepted Half a page. Never edited — superseded by a new one.
Release note What changed, what to watch, and how to roll back Five lines.
Handover / runbook page How to operate the thing when you are on holiday As short as it can be and still work.
an ADR, in the format that has outlived every alternative
# ADR-014 — Store outbox messages in the same database as the orders

## Status
Accepted, 2026-02-11. (Supersedes nothing.)

## Context
Placing an order must publish an OrderPlaced event. Writing to the database
and publishing to the broker are two systems, so a crash between them either
loses the event or publishes one for an order that does not exist.

## Decision
Write the event to an Outbox table inside the same transaction as the order.
A background dispatcher publishes and marks rows sent.

## Alternatives considered
- Publish first, then save. Rejected: an event for an order that never existed
  is worse than a late event.
- Distributed transaction across SQL Server and the broker. Rejected:
  operationally heavy, poorly supported, and it does not survive the broker
  being a different technology next year.

## Consequences
+ The order and its event are now atomic — one transaction, one outcome.
+ Duplicate delivery becomes possible, so consumers must be idempotent.
− A dispatcher process now exists and needs monitoring: alert on the age of
  the oldest undispatched row.

Two things make that worth writing. It records the alternatives, so in eighteen months nobody re-argues a settled question from scratch. And it records the consequences you accepted — including the negative one, which is exactly the thing that gets forgotten and then rediscovered as a bug.

Bug reports, and the minimal repro

Half of "troubleshooting" is turning a complaint into something reproducible. A good report has four parts and takes two minutes to write:

  1. What I did — exact steps, exact data, exact user.
  2. What I expected.
  3. What happened — including the correlation id and the timestamp.
  4. Environment — which version, which environment, which browser.

Then narrow it. Remove one variable at a time until it stops failing; the last thing you removed is the cause. This is the discipline chapter 27 calls a method for finding bugs, and it works identically on somebody else's system at three in the afternoon.

The reduction usually finds it before the debugger does

"It fails for this customer" becomes "it fails for customers with more than one shipping address" becomes "it fails when two addresses have the same postcode" — and by then you have not only reproduced it, you have named the bug and can guess the line.

Try it yourself
  1. Take the last thing that broke on your own machine and write the half-page postmortem for it, five whys included. Make yourself stop at a systemic cause rather than "I typed it wrong".
  2. Write an ADR for a decision in this repository you disagree with — the repository pattern, say — stating the alternatives honestly. Arguing the side you did not pick is the exercise.
  3. Look at the health check from chapter 25 and turn it into three symptom alerts. For each, write the first two lines of the runbook.
  4. Break something deliberately: remove a configuration key, start the app, and follow one correlation id from the error to the cause. Time yourself.
  5. Take a vague complaint — "the export is wrong" — and write the four-part bug report you wish you had been given.

Golden rules

  1. Mitigate before you diagnose. Roll back, flag off, shed load. Understanding can wait; the users cannot.
  2. If a deploy went out in the last hour, roll it back. You do not need to know which change did it.
  3. Say what is true in the channel, early and often. Half of incident management is other people knowing the current state.
  4. Escalating and being wrong costs ten minutes; not escalating and being wrong costs a day.
  5. But a team where everything is Sev 1 stops responding to Sev 1. Severity is about who is stopped and whether there is a way round.
  6. Alert on symptoms, not causes. High CPU is not an incident; a user waiting four seconds is.
  7. For queues and backlogs, alert on age, not count. A big queue that is draining is fine.
  8. Every alert needs an action. One that nobody acts on is worse than none, because it hides the one that matters.
  9. Ask for an identifier first. A correlation id turns an afternoon into five minutes.
  10. "Works on my machine" is configuration, culture, data or concurrency. Check those four before reading the code again.
  11. Postmortems are blameless because blame destroys the information. Stop the five whys at a system, never at a person.
  12. Every action item must be a change to code, configuration or process. "Be more careful" is not an action item.
  13. Write the analysis before the code and the ADR when you decide — recording the alternatives is most of the value.
  14. Reduce a bug by removing one variable at a time. The last thing you removed is the cause.

Interview questions

"Production is down. Walk me through what you do."

Confirm the symptom and how wide it is, tell people in the channel, then mitigate before diagnosing — if anything deployed recently I roll back first, otherwise flag the feature off or shed the load. Once users are working again I diagnose calmly: correlation id, logs for that request, then widen to see whether it is everyone, then correlate with what changed. Afterwards I write it up blamelessly with action items. Leading with the rollback rather than the investigation is the whole answer — it is what distinguishes someone who has been in an incident from someone who has read about one.

"How do you decide the severity?"

Not by how broken it looks, but by who is unable to do what and whether there is a workaround. Money or data being lost, or everyone stopped, is the top severity and warrants waking people. An important feature with a painful workaround is today's problem, not tonight's. And when I am unsure I escalate, because being wrong that way costs a colleague ten minutes — while being careful not to call everything critical, since a team where everything is urgent stops reacting to anything.

"What makes a good alert?"

It fires on a symptom a user would notice — error rate, latency, the age of the oldest unprocessed item — rather than on a cause like CPU, which is high during healthy batch work and normal during real outages. It has a threshold and a duration so a single blip does not page anyone, and it has an action: a runbook entry saying what it means and the first two things to try. If nobody acts on it, it should be fixed or deleted, because alerts nobody reads are how the real one gets missed.

"What is a blameless postmortem, and why bother?"

A write-up that establishes what happened and why the system allowed it, without attributing fault to a person. It is not politeness — it is the only way to get accurate information, because in a blaming culture people stop reporting and you find out later and worse. The practical test is where the five whys stop: at "options are never validated at start-up" rather than at "someone forgot the key", and every action item is a change to code, configuration or process rather than an appeal to care more.

"It works in test and fails in production. Where do you look?"

Four differences, in order: configuration — usually a missing key binding silently to a default rather than a wrong value; culture and time zone, since my machine is Italian and the container is invariant and UTC; data, because production has the customer with 400,000 orders and the null surname; and concurrency, because in test I was the only user. Then I would ask for a correlation id and read that one request end to end rather than guessing.

"What documentation do you write as a developer?"

A one-page technical analysis before starting anything non-trivial — what was asked, what it means here, what changes, what could go wrong. An ADR when a decision has alternatives worth recording, in the context/decision/consequences form, including the negative consequences we accepted. A short release note saying what to watch and how to roll back. And a runbook page for anything I would otherwise be phoned about while on holiday. Short and current beats comprehensive and stale.
