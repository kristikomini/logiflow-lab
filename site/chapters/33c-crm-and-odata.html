CRM integration, OData, and the two owners of a customer

Chapter 14b taught the mechanics of calling a system you do not own. This chapter is the specific system the adverts keep naming: a CRM — Salesforce or Dynamics 365 — which unlike a carrier API is not a service you call but a second database of your customers, edited by people, all day, while you sync it. The protocol on the wire is OData, and almost nobody who lists it on a CV can say what the $ in $filter is doing.

From the adverts

One Emilia-Romagna advert for an integration developer lists, among its requirements, “esperienza nell’integrazione di sistemi CRM, in particolare Salesforce e Microsoft Dynamics 365” and, as a separate line, “sviluppo e integrazione tramite API RESTful, servizi OData e Web Services”. Those are two bullets, not one: they want REST, and OData, and SOAP, because a company with a CRM has all three on the wire at once.

The duty list beside it is the honest description of the job — “gestione e manutenzione correttiva ed evolutiva delle integrazioni esistenti” and “collaborazione con fornitori esterni e software house”. Most of this work is inheriting somebody else's sync and being the person who can explain why it drifted.

Two systems that both believe they own the customer

🧒 Simple

Two people keep an address book for the same set of friends, in two different notebooks.

Every evening they meet and copy anything new into each other's book. That works until the evening they both changed the same friend's phone number that day — and then the question is not how do we copy faster, it is whose number is right. No amount of better copying answers it. Somebody has to decide, in advance, which notebook wins for phone numbers.

They also have to agree how to tell that two entries are the same friend, because one wrote “Rossi, M.” and the other wrote “Marco Rossi”.

🎓 Professional

An integration with a CRM is not a transport problem, it is an ownership problem. The transport is a solved, boring HTTP call. What is not solved, and what every one of these projects actually fails on, is three decisions that must be written down before any code:

  • System of record, per field. Not per system — per field. The CRM usually owns the commercial data (owner, stage, opportunity value); the ERP usually owns the fiscal data (VAT number, credit limit, invoice address). A project that says “the CRM is master” without qualifying it has not made the decision, it has postponed it.
  • The matching key. Two systems that invented their own identifiers need a third thing that means “the same company”. In Italy the honest candidate is the partita IVA or the codice fiscale — and both are empty on a prospect who has not signed anything yet, which is exactly the record a salesperson creates first.
  • What a delete means. A row deleted in the CRM is almost never a row to delete in the ERP, which has invoices pointing at it. Nearly always the answer is a status flag, not a DELETE.

Say those three sentences in an interview and you have said more than most candidates with the word Salesforce on their CV, because they learned a portal and you learned the problem.

OData: a query language you did not write, over HTTP

🧒 Simple

An ordinary REST endpoint is a vending machine: press the button, get the thing behind it. If you want half the thing, or the things in a different order, somebody has to build another button.

OData is a vending machine you can talk to. Same machine, but you can say “only the ones from Modena, only their names, the first twenty, newest first” — and nobody had to add a button for that particular sentence.

🎓 Professional

OData is a standard that fixes what REST deliberately leaves open: how a client asks for filtering, sorting, paging, projection and related data, and how a service describes its own shape. Every OData service uses the same query words, so a client written against one can be pointed at another. That is why Microsoft put it under Dataverse, Business Central and SharePoint alike.

It arrives in two directions, and the advert means both: consuming a CRM's OData feed, and exposing one from your own ASP.NET service so the CRM (or a Power BI report, which is the usual real answer) can query you. 

GET /api/data/v9.2/accounts
      ?$select=name,revenue,modifiedon
      &$filter=statecode eq 0 and modifiedon gt 2026-09-01T00:00:00Z
      &$expand=primarycontactid($select=fullname,emailaddress1)
      &$orderby=modifiedon asc
      &$top=100
      &$count=true

200 OK
{
  "@odata.context": ".../$metadata#accounts(name,revenue,modifiedon)",
  "@odata.count": 4271,
  "value": [ { "name": "Ferrari Meccanica srl", "revenue": 1840000.0, ... } ],
  "@odata.nextLink": ".../accounts?$select=...&$skiptoken=%3Ccookie%20pagenumber..."
}
Line by line
  • $select — the single highest-value habit in this chapter. Without it Dynamics returns every column of every row, which on an account table carrying three hundred customised fields is roughly forty times the bytes you needed. A slow integration is usually this and nothing else.
  • $filter=statecode eq 0 — the operators are words, not symbols: eq ne gt ge lt le, joined with and or not, plus functions such as contains(name,'Rossi') and startswith. This is not SQL and it will not accept SQL.
  • Dates are literals with no quotes and must be UTC with the trailing Z. Quoting one gives a type error you will fix in a minute; sending a local time gives no error at all and silently shifts your delta window by two hours in summer.
  • $expand — the join. Note the nested $select inside the parentheses: without it you have just pulled every column of the related record too, and undone the first $select.
  • @odata.count is the total matching the filter, not the number returned. People use it to size a progress bar and then reuse it as a loop bound, which is the wrong number.
  • @odata.nextLink is the whole paging story, and it is server-driven. You do not compute the next page: if that property is present there is more, and you GET the URL exactly as given. Dataverse caps a page at 5000 rows whatever your $top says, so a client that ignores the next link quietly processes the first 5000 customers and reports success.
  • $skiptoken is opaque — a cursor, not an offset. Do not parse it, do not rebuild it, and do not assume $skip is the same thing: on a table being edited while you read it, offset paging skips and duplicates rows, and cursor paging does not.

GET /api/data/v9.2/$metadata returns the service's own schema as XML — every entity set, every property, every type and relationship. That is what a code generator or a Power Query connector reads, and it is the fastest way to answer “what is that field actually called” when the CRM's own screen says Ragione sociale and the API wants name.

Exposing OData, and the line that makes it safe

// Program.cs — Microsoft.AspNetCore.OData
var model = new ODataConventionModelBuilder();
model.EntitySet<ShipmentDto>("Shipments");

builder.Services.AddControllers().AddOData(opt => opt
    .AddRouteComponents("odata", model.GetEdmModel())
    .Select().Filter().OrderBy().Count()   // note what is NOT here: .Expand()
    .SetMaxTop(500));                      // a client asking for more gets 500

[EnableQuery(MaxExpansionDepth = 1, MaxAnyAllExpressionDepth = 1)]
public IQueryable<ShipmentDto> Get() => db.Shipments.AsNoTracking();
Line by line
  • [EnableQuery] means an outside caller writes your WHERE clause. The query options are translated into the IQueryable and reach the database as SQL. That is the feature, and it is also the entire risk.
  • .Select().Filter().OrderBy().Count() — an allow-list. Every option you do not name is rejected rather than honoured, which is the correct default and the opposite of the sample code on most blogs.
  • .Expand() is deliberately absent. Nested expands are how one innocent GET becomes a join across four tables; enable it when a real client needs it, and bound it with MaxExpansionDepth when you do.
  • SetMaxTop(500) — without it $top is unbounded, and so is your memory. No $top at all is worse: it means every row.
  • AsNoTracking() — the query is read-only and never saved, so the EF Core change tracker is pure cost. The same reasoning as chapter 12.
  • Return a DTO, never the entity. $select over an entity exposes every column you ever add to it, including the one you add next year without thinking about this endpoint. It is chapter 15's over-posting argument, pointed the other way.

Dynamics 365: it is Dataverse underneath, and the names lie

🎓 Professional

“Dynamics 365” in an advert is two unrelated products. Getting this wrong in the first five minutes of an interview is a real risk, because the vocabulary is shared and the skills are not:

  • Dynamics 365 Business Central — the ERP, the old Navision line. You extend it in AL. That is chapter 33.
  • Dynamics 365 Customer Engagement (Sales, Customer Service, Field Service) — the CRM, the old CRM 4.0 line. It stores everything in Dataverse, and you integrate with it in C#. That is this chapter.

When an advert pairs Dynamics 365 with Salesforce, it means the CRM one. When it pairs it with gestionale or ERP, it means Business Central. 

// Microsoft.PowerPlatform.Dataverse.Client — a supported, typed client.
// Register it as a SINGLETON: it holds the token cache and a pooled handler.
var svc = new ServiceClient(
    $"AuthType=ClientSecret;Url={url};ClientId={id};ClientSecret={secret}");

// Upsert on an ALTERNATE KEY — the whole integration pattern in one call.
var account = new Entity("account", "logiflow_erpcode", "C-004182")
{
    ["name"]             = "Ferrari Meccanica srl",
    ["revenue"]          = new Money(1_840_000m),
    ["primarycontactid"] = new EntityReference("contact", contactId),
};
await svc.UpsertAsync(new UpsertRequest { Target = account });

// The same thing over the raw Web API, which is what you write when the SDK
// is not an option:
//   PATCH /api/data/v9.2/accounts(logiflow_erpcode='C-004182')
//   If-Match: *                    ← update only, never create
//   { "name": "Ferrari Meccanica srl",
//     "primarycontactid@odata.bind": "/contacts(0f2b…)" }   ← setting a lookup
Line by line
  • An alternate key is the feature that makes CRM sync sane. You register logiflow_erpcode as a key on the account table, and from then on you address records by your identifier. No lookup-then-decide, no storing their GUID in your database, no race between two workers creating the same customer twice.
  • UpsertAsync — one round trip, and idempotent. Replaying the whole batch after a crash is safe, which is the property chapter 14b spent its length arguing for.
  • logiflow_ is a publisher prefix. Every custom field and table in Dataverse carries one. A field the CRM screen labels Codice ERP is logiflow_erpcode on the wire — and the label is translatable while the name is not, so never key anything off a label.
  • new Money(...) and EntityReference — Dataverse has its own attribute types. A currency is not a decimal, a lookup is not a Guid, and a dropdown is an OptionSetValue wrapping an int. Assigning the bare primitive fails at runtime, not at compile time.
  • @odata.bind is how a relationship is set over the Web API — a URL to the related record, on a property with that suffix. Posting the bare GUID into primarycontactid gives a type error that does not mention the suffix, and it costs everybody an hour exactly once.
  • If-Match: * forces update-only; If-None-Match: * forces create-only; neither header means upsert. Choosing deliberately is what stops a bug in your matching logic from creating three thousand duplicate accounts overnight.
  • Singleton ServiceClient. Building one per call redoes the OAuth handshake and the metadata download every time — the same lifetime mistake as new HttpClient() in chapter 14b, wearing a Microsoft badge.
Service protection limits, and why 429 is not an error

Dataverse enforces per-user, per-server limits — on the order of 6000 requests, 20 minutes of execution time and 52 concurrent requests inside a five-minute sliding window. Cross one and you get 429 Too Many Requests with a Retry-After header. That is not a failure, it is flow control, and the correct response is to sleep exactly that long and carry on. ServiceClient does it for you; a hand-rolled HttpClient does not, and a retry policy with a fixed delay that ignores the header will simply be throttled again, forever.

The architectural consequence: bulk work goes through ExecuteMultipleRequest or OData $batch in batches of a few hundred, not through a foreach over fifty thousand rows.

Line by line
  • JWT bearer is the flow for a service with no human present. Your private key signs an assertion, Salesforce verifies it against the certificate uploaded to the Connected App, and you get a token. Nothing stores a user's password, which is why this is the one to name in an interview — the older username-password flow still works in places and is the wrong answer.
  • instance_url is returned, not configured. Hard-coding acme.my.salesforce.com works for a year and breaks during an org migration, at which point nobody remembers the login response was telling you the answer all along.
  • /services/data/v61.0/ — the version is in the path and Salesforce retires old ones on a published schedule. That is a dated dependency, not a constant: put it in configuration.
  • The upsert-by-External-Id URL is the entire pattern. Your identifier is in the path, so the call is idempotent and needs no prior query. It is the Dataverse alternate key again, and both vendors built it because everybody was writing query-then-branch and creating duplicates on the retry.
  • 204 versus 201 is the only way you learn which happened. Both are success. Code that treats a non-200 as failure reports every update as an error; code that ignores the difference cannot say how many customers it just created, which is the number somebody will ask for.
  • A governor limit is not a rate limit. A rate limit says slower; a governor limit says this transaction is over, roll back, counting queries, rows and CPU inside one Apex transaction. It is why volume goes through the Bulk API rather than twenty thousand single calls, and why a Salesforce developer flinches at a loop with a query inside it.

Delta sync, and the clock you do not own

🧒 Simple

You copy the changes made since last time by writing down what time it was when you finished.

The trouble is that you are reading their clock, not yours, and someone can be halfway through writing an entry at the exact moment you look. You saw everything up to two o'clock — except the line that was still being written just before two and landed just after, with the earlier time on it. Next time you start from two o'clock, and that line is never copied by anybody, ever.

🎓 Professional

The watermark pattern is right, and it has three details that are always got wrong. You store the last-seen modification timestamp, and next run you ask for modifiedon gt @watermark in Dataverse, or SystemModstamp in Salesforce — not LastModifiedDate, which does not move when an automated process touches the row.

  1. Use their clock, never yours. Take the new watermark from the maximum timestamp in the rows you actually received. DateTime.UtcNow on your server is a different clock with a different skew, and the gap between them is a window of rows that nobody will ever fetch again.
  2. Overlap deliberately. Re-read a few minutes behind the watermark on every run. Transactions commit out of timestamp order, so a strictly-greater window loses rows. The overlap costs nothing precisely because every write is an upsert.
  3. Order ascending by the field you filtered on. Paging a filtered set with no $orderby gives no stable order, so rows move between pages while you read and you get duplicates and holes in the same run.

Deletes are invisible to a watermark by construction — the row is gone, so it cannot appear in a query. Dataverse can log them for you through change tracking; Salesforce keeps them in queryAll and the Recycle Bin for a couple of weeks. If neither is switched on, the only honest answer is a periodic reconciliation of identifiers, and the time to say that is during analysis, not during the incident.

Polling, webhooks, and the queue in between

polling          CRM ◀──every 5 min: "anything new?"── you
                 simple, restartable, same load at 3 a.m. as at noon
                 latency = the interval. Fine for a nightly ERP sync.

webhook/event    CRM ──"account 4182 changed"──▶ you
                 near-instant, and now THEIR retry policy is YOUR uptime
                 requirement. Miss the delivery window and it is gone.

the shape that survives being on call:

    CRM ──event──▶ [ validate, enqueue, return 202 ] ──▶ queue
                                                          │
                                       worker ◀───────────┘
                                       re-fetches the CURRENT record, upserts
Line by line
  • The receiving endpoint does no work. It authenticates the caller, writes the event to a queue and returns 202 Accepted in milliseconds. Every retry policy on the other side is built for a fast endpoint; doing the sync inline turns a slow database into duplicate deliveries.
  • Treat the event as a doorbell, not a parcel. Take the record id from it and re-fetch the current state. Events arrive out of order and get redelivered, and applying a stale payload from a duplicate delivery reverts a newer change — a bug that presents as “the CRM keeps undoing my edits” and takes a week to be believed.
  • The queue is what makes it restartable. With it, a deployment or a database failover costs nothing; without it, every event that arrived during the restart is simply lost. That is chapter 22's argument, unchanged.
  • Authenticate the webhook. A public endpoint that writes into your ERP because somebody POSTed a record id is a vulnerability, not an integration. Dataverse can post through Azure Service Bus with a key; Salesforce signs its notifications. Use whichever exists rather than trusting the shape of the payload.
  • Say the trade-off out loud: polling is slower and nearly impossible to break, push is instant and puts you on the hook for availability. Most integrations should poll, and the ones that should not are the ones a human is standing there waiting for.

The SOAP you will still meet

The advert says Web Services next to API RESTful, and that is not padding. A company with a CRM and an ERP older than it has SOAP somewhere: Salesforce Outbound Messaging is SOAP, Business Central still publishes SOAP endpoints beside its OData ones, and half the courier and banking integrations in Italy are a WSDL somebody emailed you a decade ago.

Conceptually there is nothing to learn — it is a procedure call over HTTP in an XML envelope, with a machine-readable contract. Practically: in Visual Studio, Add › Connected Service › WCF Web Service, or dotnet-svcutil at the command line, generates a typed client from the WSDL. Two things are worth knowing before you touch one. The generated client derives from ClientBase, which is not pooled the way HttpClient is and must be closed — and its Dispose throws when the channel is faulted, so a plain using replaces the real exception with a CommunicationObjectFaultedException and hides what went wrong. And generated code is not source you maintain: regenerate it when the WSDL changes, and keep your own logic in a wrapper beside it.

Golden rules

  1. Decide the system of record per field, not per system, and write it down before any code. Everything else in a CRM integration follows from that table.
  2. Agree the matching key first, and assume it is empty on exactly the records the salespeople create.
  3. A delete on one side is a status change on the other, until somebody senior says otherwise in writing.
  4. Always $select. Without it you fetch every column of every custom field anyone ever added.
  5. Follow @odata.nextLink until it is absent. A client that reads the first page reports success and syncs a fraction of the data.
  6. A skip token is a cursor, not an offset. Never rebuild it, and never page a changing table by $skip.
  7. OData dates are unquoted and UTC. A local time throws nothing and shifts your whole delta window.
  8. When you expose OData, allow-list the query options, cap $top, leave $expand off until asked and project to a DTO, because [EnableQuery] lets the caller write your WHERE clause.
  9. Dynamics 365 is two products. The CRM is Customer Engagement on Dataverse and you write C#; the ERP is Business Central and you write AL.
  10. Address records by your own identifier — a Dataverse alternate key or a Salesforce External Id — and upsert. Query-then-branch is what creates the duplicates.
  11. Set a Dataverse lookup with @odata.bind and a URL, never a bare GUID.
  12. ServiceClient is a singleton. Per-call construction redoes the handshake and the metadata download every time.
  13. 429 with Retry-After is flow control, not a failure. Honour the header; a fixed retry delay is throttled forever.
  14. A governor limit is not a rate limit — it ends the transaction rather than slowing it. Volume belongs in the bulk API.
  15. Take the watermark from their timestamps, overlap the window, and order ascending by the field you filtered on.
  16. Deletes never appear in a watermark query. Turn on change tracking or plan a reconciliation, and raise it during analysis.
  17. A webhook is a doorbell: validate, enqueue, return 202, and re-fetch the current record in a worker.
  18. Never apply an event's payload directly. Redelivery and out-of-order arrival let a stale payload overwrite a newer change.
  19. Poll unless a human is waiting. Push makes their retry policy your uptime requirement.
  20. Regenerate SOAP clients from the WSDL, never hand-edit them, and close the channel explicitly rather than with using.

Interview questions

"We need to sync customers between our ERP and Salesforce. Where do you start?"

Not with the API. With three decisions: which system owns which field — commercial data in the CRM, fiscal data in the ERP, usually — what key means “the same company” in both, and what a delete on one side should do on the other. Once those are written down the code is an upsert on an external identifier and a watermark query, and it takes a fraction of the time. If they are not written down, the integration works in test and produces duplicates and arguments in production.

"What is OData, and how is it different from REST?"

REST is an architectural style and leaves querying to each API's own invention. OData is a standard on top of it: the same words for filtering, projection, sorting, paging and expanding related records, plus a $metadata document describing the service's shape. The benefit is that a client written for one OData service works against another, which is why Microsoft put it under Dataverse, Business Central and SharePoint. The risk, when you expose one, is that the caller is writing your database query.

"Your sync pulls 5000 accounts and says it is done, but the CRM has 40,000. What happened?"

Almost certainly @odata.nextLink is being ignored. Dataverse caps a page at 5000 rows regardless of $top and returns a next link when there is more, so the fix is a loop that follows that link verbatim until it is absent — never a bigger $top. I would also check the query is ordered ascending on the field being filtered, because paging an unordered changing set loses rows in a subtler way that looks like the same bug.

"How do you avoid creating duplicates when a job is retried?"

Make the write idempotent by addressing the record with my own identifier. In Dataverse that is an alternate key and an upsert request; in Salesforce it is an External Id field and a PATCH to the sObject path with my code in the URL. One round trip, no query-then-decide, and re-running the whole batch after a crash changes nothing. The duplicates people get come from checking whether a record exists and then creating it — two calls, with a window in between that two workers can both be inside.

"You are getting 429s from Dataverse. What do you do?"

Read Retry-After and wait exactly that long. It is service protection rather than an error — there is a request count, an execution-time budget and a concurrency cap over a five-minute window. A fixed-delay retry ignores the header and gets throttled again immediately. Then I would fix the cause rather than the symptom: batch the writes a few hundred at a time, and check I am not making one call per row where one call per page would do.

"How would you pick up only what changed since the last run?"

A watermark on their modification timestamp — modifiedon in Dataverse, SystemModstamp in Salesforce, because LastModifiedDate does not move for automated updates. Three details matter: the watermark comes from the maximum timestamp in the rows I received rather than from my own clock, I overlap the window by a few minutes because transactions commit out of timestamp order, and I order ascending by the same field I filter on so paging is stable. Deletes cannot appear in that query at all, so they need change tracking or a periodic reconciliation of identifiers — and I would raise that during analysis rather than discover it.

"Webhook or polling?"

Polling, unless somebody is waiting for the result. It is restartable, the load is predictable and nothing is lost during a deployment. A webhook is instant and turns the other system's retry policy into my uptime requirement. When I do take events the endpoint authenticates, enqueues and returns 202, and a worker re-fetches the current record — I treat the event as a doorbell rather than a parcel, because redelivery and out-of-order arrival otherwise let a stale payload overwrite a newer change.

"Have you worked with Salesforce?"

Not inside it — I have not written Apex or built Lightning components, and I would not claim it. What I have done is the integration side, which is what this role is: JWT bearer for a server with no user present, REST against sObjects, SOQL, upsert on an External Id, the bulk API for volume, and the limits that shape all of it. The concepts carry across to Dataverse almost one for one; what changes is the nouns.
