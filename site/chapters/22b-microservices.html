Microservices, honestly

The word is in the adverts, so you need to be able to talk about it. What follows is the version that survives contact with a fifty-person company: what the architecture actually buys, what it costs, why the answer for most teams here is not yet — and how to say that without sounding like you are avoiding the question.

From the adverts

"Partecipazione a progetti legati ad AI, automazione, database, integrazioni e microservizi", and again in the nice-to-haves as "interesse per architetture a microservizi". Note the register: interesse. Nobody is asking a junior to design a service mesh.

You already have the hard parts — chapter 22 for messaging, chapter 23 for boundaries, chapter 14b for calling things you do not own. This chapter is the vocabulary and the judgement that sit on top.

What it actually buys, and what it costs

🧒 Simple

One big kitchen: everyone cooks in the same room. It is easy to pass the salt. But when someone drops a pan, everybody stops, and you can only redecorate when everyone agrees to stop cooking.

Many small kitchens: each team has their own. A dropped pan bothers one room. But now the salt has to be carried down a corridor, sometimes it does not arrive, and someone has to maintain the corridors. If your restaurant has four cooks, the corridors are the whole problem and there was never anything wrong with the room.

🎓 The honest ledger
You gainYou pay
Independent deployment. One team ships without coordinating with four others. This is the real benefit and almost the only one that survives scrutiny. Every call can now fail. A method call became a network call: timeouts, retries, partial failure. Chapter 14b, everywhere.
Independent scaling. The one component under load scales alone. No more transactions across the boundary. Sagas and compensation replace COMMIT.
Fault isolation — if you did the work to make it true. No more joins. Data you used to join is now in someone else's database.
Technology choice per service. Rarely worth much, frequently cited. Operational tax: deployment, discovery, config, distributed tracing, and local development that no longer means pressing F5.

Read the left column again. Every entry is about teams, not code. Microservices are an organisational solution — they let independent groups ship independently. That is why they pay off at fifteen teams and hurt at two: with one team you have bought every cost and none of the benefit.

The spectrum, and where a company here actually sits

A modular monolith is what this repository is (chapter 23). The boundaries are real and enforced — the architecture tests fail the build if Domain references Infrastructure — but it deploys as one thing and commits as one transaction. If those modules later need to become services, the seams already exist, which is precisely what makes the split possible.

⚠ The distributed monolith

The common failure: services split by layer or by table rather than by business capability, so every user action touches four of them and they must be released together. You now have network calls, eventual consistency, four pipelines and four sets of logs — and still cannot deploy anything independently.

The test: can you deploy one service on a Tuesday afternoon without telling anyone? If not, they are not microservices, whatever the diagram says.

How you cut: by capability, never by layer

✗ Wrong seams✓ Right seams
A "database service", an "API service", a "UI service"Ordering, Shipping, Invoicing, Catalogue
One service per table — a Customer service, an Order serviceOne service per thing the business does
Whatever fits the current team's skillsWhere the language changes: a "shipment" means something different to the warehouse than to finance
🎓 The seam is where the vocabulary changes

This is the DDD idea of a bounded context, and it is more practical than it sounds. If the warehouse says "order" and means a picking list, and finance says "order" and means an invoiceable event, you have found a boundary — the two need different models of the same word, and forcing them to share one produces a class with forty properties that nobody understands.

The corollary that surprises people: duplicating data across that boundary is correct, not a normalisation failure. Shipping keeps its own copy of the customer address it needs. It is not a cache to be invalidated; it is Shipping's own record of where it was told to deliver.

Synchronous or asynchronous?

Synchronous — HTTP, gRPCAsynchronous — queue or event
Use whenThe caller cannot continue without the answerThe caller only needs it to happen eventually
CouplingTemporal: they must be up right nowOnly to the message shape
FailureYours becomes theirs, immediatelyAbsorbed by the queue
CostLatency adds up along the chainEventual consistency, and a UI that must show it
⚠ The synchronous chain is how one outage becomes four

If A calls B calls C calls D, then A's availability is the product of all four, and its latency is the sum. Four services at 99.9% give you 99.6% — roughly three hours a month of downtime that no single team caused.

So the default for anything that is not "I need the answer to render this page" is an event. "Order placed" published once, and Shipping and Invoicing react in their own time — which is the outbox pattern from chapter 22, and the reason this repository has one.

The tax: what you need before you can run them

This is the list that decides whether a company is ready, and being able to recite it is what makes the "not yet" answer credible rather than lazy:

  • Automated deployment per service. If releasing takes a person and a checklist, ten services means ten times the ceremony — and independent deployment was the entire point.
  • Distributed tracing. Without a correlation id crossing every boundary, a slow request becomes unattributable. Chapter 25, now mandatory rather than nice.
  • Centralised logging. Nobody can SSH into six containers.
  • A local development story. If a developer needs six services running to fix one bug, productivity falls off a cliff. .NET Aspire exists largely to answer this.
  • Contract discipline. Someone else depends on your JSON. Additive changes only, versioned endpoints, and consumer-driven tests.
  • A way to answer "where is order 4711 stuck?" across services. Design that in before you need it at 09:00 on a Monday.

Scheduled work, and the second instance

Automation and background jobs travel with this territory — and the moment there is more than one instance of your service, a scheduled job becomes a distributed-systems problem.

the job that runs twice
// This is correct, simple, and the right default for scheduled work in .NET —
// no extra dependency, hosted by the same process, cancelled cleanly on
// shutdown. Chapter 22's outbox dispatcher is exactly this.
public sealed class NightlyReconciliation(IServiceScopeFactory scopes)
    : BackgroundService
{
    protected override async Task ExecuteAsync(CancellationToken ct)
    {
        using var timer = new PeriodicTimer(TimeSpan.FromMinutes(5));
        while (await timer.WaitForNextTickAsync(ct))
        {
            // ⚠ Scale to three replicas and this runs three times, concurrently.
            //   Nobody notices until the day it sends three emails, or
            //   double-posts a ledger entry.
            using var scope = scopes.CreateScope();
            await Reconcile(scope, ct);
        }
    }
}
🎓 The three answers, in order of preference
  1. Make the work idempotent and let it run twice. The cheapest fix, and it is the one that keeps working when a lock expires at the wrong moment. Claim rows with an UPDATE … WHERE Status = 'Pending' and act only on the rows you claimed — the same trick the outbox dispatcher uses.
  2. Take a distributed lock — a row in SQL Server with an expiry, or a Redis key (chapter 20). Workable, but remember that a lock with a timeout is a lease: your process can pause for a garbage collection and wake up believing it still holds a lock that has expired. Which is why point 1 is still true underneath.
  3. Take it out of the service. A scheduler that runs the job once — Hangfire or Quartz.NET with a shared store, a Kubernetes CronJob, an Azure Function on a timer. The right answer when the schedule is complex, needs a UI, or must survive the web app being scaled to zero.

Hangfire and Quartz both add a dependency, a database schema and an operational surface. Reach for them when BackgroundService genuinely stops being enough — retry history, an admin dashboard, cron expressions maintained by someone who is not you — and not before.

"We're moving to microservices"

You will hear this in an interview. It is a chance to show judgement, and the useful response is two questions rather than an opinion:

  1. "Which contexts have you identified?" If there is an answer — ordering, shipping, invoicing — they have done the thinking. If the answer is "we're splitting the API from the database", they have not, and you have learned the most important thing about the role.
  2. "What does deploying one of them look like today?" This is the readiness question in disguise. If the answer involves a person and a checklist, the split will make everything harder, and saying so gently is exactly the seniority the advert is asking for.

And when asked how you would split an existing monolith: the same strangler answer as chapter 16b. Put a gateway in front, extract the least-coupled context first — something on the edge, with its own data and few callers, like notifications or reporting — prove the whole path works end to end, and only then take on something that shares data with the core. Never extract the domain core first; it is the hardest and it is where a failed migration strands you.

Try it yourself
  1. Look at this repository's projects and name three bounded contexts inside it. For each, ask what data it would need its own copy of — that answer is the real cost of splitting.
  2. Take one existing use case and write down every network call it would need if the modules were services. Multiply four availabilities of 99.9% together and look at the number.
  3. Find the outbox dispatcher and work out what breaks if two instances run it at once. Then find the line that already prevents it.
  4. Write a BackgroundService with a PeriodicTimer that appends to a file, run two copies, and watch the duplicates. Then make the work idempotent and run two copies again.
  5. Argue the opposite case out loud: you have four developers and one deployment that takes twenty minutes. What would splitting into six services actually improve?

Golden rules

  1. Microservices are an organisational solution. They buy independent deployment for independent teams; with one team you pay every cost and get none of it.
  2. The default is a modular monolith. Real enforced boundaries, one deployable, one transaction — most of the benefit, almost none of the tax.
  3. Cut by business capability, never by layer or by table. The seam is where the vocabulary changes.
  4. If they must be released together, they are a distributed monolith. The test is deploying one on a Tuesday without telling anyone.
  5. Duplicating data across a boundary is correct. It is that service's own record, not a cache.
  6. Prefer events to synchronous calls. A chain multiplies availability downwards and sums latency upwards.
  7. Every network call can fail, and a timeout means unknown. Chapter 14b applies inside your own system now.
  8. You lose transactions and joins at the boundary. Sagas with compensation replace rollback; that is the real cost.
  9. You need deployment automation, tracing, central logs and a local dev story first. Without them the split makes everything worse.
  10. More than one instance means a scheduled job runs more than once. Make it idempotent first; a lock is a lease and can expire under you.
  11. Reach for Hangfire or Quartz when BackgroundService stops being enough — not before.
  12. Extract the least-coupled context first. Never start with the domain core.

Interview questions

"What are the advantages of microservices?"

Independent deployment and independent scaling, plus fault isolation if you do the work to make it real. But the honest framing is that these are organisational benefits: they let separate teams ship without coordinating. The costs are technical and land on everyone — every call can fail, transactions and joins stop working across boundaries, and you take on deployment, tracing and local-development problems you did not have. Worth it at fifteen teams, usually not at two.

"Should we split our monolith into microservices?"

I would ask what problem we are solving. If it is that teams block each other on releases, then yes, and the split should follow team boundaries. If it is that the code is tangled, a modular monolith fixes that far more cheaply — you get enforced boundaries without the network in between, and the seams you create are exactly what makes a later split possible. And before splitting anything I would want automated per-service deployment, distributed tracing and central logging, because without them the split makes every problem harder to diagnose.

"How would you decide where the service boundaries go?"

By business capability, using bounded contexts — ordering, shipping, invoicing — not by technical layer and not one per table. The practical signal is where the vocabulary changes: if "order" means a picking list to the warehouse and an invoiceable event to finance, that is a boundary. And each side keeps its own copy of the data it needs, which is not a normalisation failure but that service's own record.

"What is a distributed monolith?"

Services that were split badly — usually by layer or by table — so every change touches several of them and they have to be released together. You have paid for the network, the eventual consistency, the extra pipelines and the extra logs, and you still cannot deploy independently, which was the only real benefit. The test is whether you could deploy one on a Tuesday afternoon without telling anyone.

"How do services communicate?"

Synchronously over HTTP or gRPC when the caller cannot proceed without the answer, and asynchronously through events when it only needs to happen eventually. I would default to events for anything that is not needed to render the current response, because a synchronous chain multiplies availabilities and sums latencies — four services at 99.9% is 99.6%. The reliable way to publish is the outbox: write the event in the same transaction as the data, and let a dispatcher publish it.

"You have a nightly job in a service that now runs three replicas. What happens?"

It runs three times, concurrently, and nobody notices until it sends three emails or double-posts a ledger entry. The best fix is to make the work idempotent — claim rows with a conditional update and act only on what you claimed — because that keeps working even when a lock expires at the wrong moment. A distributed lock in SQL Server or Redis is the next option, remembering it is really a lease. And if the schedule needs history, retries or a dashboard, move it out to Hangfire, Quartz or a platform scheduler.
