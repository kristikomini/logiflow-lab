The interview

The last chapter, and the one the other twenty-one exist for. This is the advert read from the other side of the table: what they will ask, how the process runs in Italy, and what you should be asking them.

From the advert

All of it — now as a checklist someone is working through while looking at you.

How the process usually runs

StageWhoWhat is actually being tested
1. Screening call (20–30 min)HR or a recruiter Availability, notice period, expected RAL, work authorisation, Italian level. Rarely technical. Have your numbers ready — hesitating here reads as unprepared.
2. Technical interview (60–90 min)A senior developer or team lead The chapters of this site. Often conversational rather than a quiz, and often with a screen share.
3. PracticalThe same person, or a pair A take-home exercise, or live coding. Sometimes a walk through your code.
4. FinalManager, sometimes the owner Fit, motivation, and the offer. In smaller Italian companies stages 3 and 4 are frequently the same meeting.

For a 1–3 year role at an SME, expect two or three meetings over two to four weeks. If a company runs six rounds for a junior position, that is information about the company.

🎓 The answer structure that works

Most candidates give a definition and stop, and the interviewer has to keep digging. Use three beats instead:

  1. The definition — one or two sentences, no hedging.
  2. The consequence — why it matters, what breaks without it.
  3. The experience — "I hit this when…" or "the trap is…"

Beat three is what separates candidates. "AsNoTracking skips change tracking" is beat one. "…so read-only queries use less memory, and I now default to projecting into a DTO instead, which gets it for free and fetches fewer columns" is all three.

The live coding round

🧒 Simple

They are not really watching whether you get the right answer. They are watching what you do when you are stuck — because that is what you will be doing on most days of the job.

Do:

  • Restate the problem before typing. "So: given a list of orders, return the top three customers by total. Should ties be broken somehow?" Thirty seconds, and it prevents solving the wrong problem in silence.
  • Think out loud. Silence is unreadable; an interviewer cannot give credit for reasoning they cannot hear.
  • Write the ugly version first, say it is ugly, then improve it. A working solution plus "this is O(n×m), let me index the customers into a dictionary" is a better performance than an elegant answer that never compiles.
  • Ask about edge cases — empty input, nulls, duplicates. It reads as exactly the capacità di analisi the advert asked for.
  • Test it by hand. Walk one input through your own code out loud.

Do not:

  • Go quiet for four minutes.
  • Pretend to know an API you do not. "I would check the exact signature, but it is roughly GroupBy then OrderByDescending" is completely fine.
  • Optimise before it works.
  • Argue with a hint. A hint is help, and refusing it is the worst possible signal.
the three exercises that come up most
// 1. Group and rank — the C# twin of the T-SQL question in chapter 10
var top = orders
    .GroupBy(o => o.CustomerId)
    .Select(g => new { CustomerId = g.Key, Total = g.Sum(o => o.Total) })
    .OrderByDescending(x => x.Total)
    .Take(3)
    .ToList();

// 2. Find the duplicates — tests whether you reach for a hash set
var duplicates = items
    .GroupBy(i => i.Code)
    .Where(g => g.Count() > 1)
    .Select(g => g.Key);

// 3. "Make this faster" — the O(n²) from chapter 06, every time
var byId = customers.ToDictionary(c => c.Id);      // index once…
foreach (var o in orders) { var c = byId[o.CustomerId]; }   // …then O(1)
Line by line
  • The same two questions, in C# and in T-SQL. This block is deliberately the twin of chapter 10's, because interviewers ask one, then the other, to see whether you know which side of the wire the work should happen on.
  • .GroupBy(o => o.CustomerId).Select(g => ...) — g.Key is the grouped value; g is enumerable, so g.Sum(...) aggregates within the group.
  • new { CustomerId = ..., Total = ... } — an anonymous type, usable inside the method and not returnable from it.
  • .OrderByDescending(...).Take(3) — order then take, never the reverse. And mention the tie-break: without a secondary sort the result is non-deterministic when totals are equal.
  • .GroupBy(i => i.Code).Where(g => g.Count() > 1) — the duplicates question, and it is really asking whether you reach for hashing. GroupBy is hash-based, so this is O(n) where the nested loop is O(n²).
  • Say where it runs. On an IQueryable this becomes SQL; on a List it runs in your process. Volunteering that distinction unprompted is the single most effective thing you can do in a .NET interview, and it is chapter 06's rule arriving where it counts.

⚠ On salary numbers

Do not walk into a negotiation carrying figures from a website. Ranges for a 1–3 year .NET developer in Modena or Bologna move year to year and vary widely by sector, CCNL, company size and contract type. Check current data close to the interview — Osservatorio JobPricing, Glassdoor and Indeed for Italy, LinkedIn Salary, and the minimums in the relevant CCNL — and cross-check with anyone you know locally.

Module 17 in this repository holds market detail and a twelve-week plan; its salary figures are explicitly flagged as approximate and needing verification, and should be treated that way.

When asked for your expectation, give a range in RAL, say it is negotiable on the whole package, and ask what range they have budgeted for the livello. Asking back is normal and expected.

What to ask them

"No questions" is the worst answer available. These are the ones that tell you whether you want the job — and they signal seniority, which is the secondary benefit.

  • "What does the codebase look like — how much is .NET Framework, how much is modern .NET, and is there active migration work?" (You have already guessed from the advert. Confirming it is impressive.)
  • "How does a change reach production? Is it automated, and how often do you deploy?" (Tells you more about engineering culture than any other question.)
  • "Are there tests, and does the build run them?"
  • "Who decides what gets built? Is there an analyst, or do developers talk to users directly?"
  • "How is code review handled? Is it required?"
  • "What would my first three months look like — and who would I be sitting with?"
  • "Is there budget or time for training and certification?"
  • "Why is the position open?" (Growth, or somebody left. Both answers are informative.)
  • "How much on-site? What is the smart-working policy?" (Hybrid is common; fully remote is less so in this sector. Ask directly.)
The red flags worth listening for

No version control question they can answer confidently. No tests, and no discomfort about it. "We deploy by copying files onto the server." Deployment only at night or at weekends. Nobody can describe the review process. A team where everyone joined less than a year ago. And the biggest one: an interviewer who cannot say what you would work on in the first month.

The week before

  1. Redo the sheet from chapter 00. ✓ / ~ / ✗ against every line of the advert. Spend your time on the ✗ and the ~.
  2. Say the starred answers out loud. Not read — said. The gap between knowing something and being able to say it fluently under mild stress is real, and only speaking closes it.
  3. Prepare three stories: something you built and are proud of, something that went wrong and what you changed, and a disagreement you handled well. Almost every behavioural question is one of these three wearing a hat.
  4. Re-read your own code — whatever you might be asked to walk through. Being unable to explain your own project is fatal, and more common than it sounds.
  5. Check the logistics. Where, how long, who, what platform. In Emilia-Romagna the office may be in a zona industriale that is thirty minutes from the station and badly served by buses.
  6. Prepare the first ninety seconds in Italian if the interview will be in Italian. The opening answer sets your confidence for the entire conversation.
Try it yourself — the final exercise

Set a timer for forty-five minutes. Take the twenty starred questions in the table above, and answer each one out loud, in three beats: definition, consequence, experience. Record yourself.

Then listen back. You are checking three specific things: whether you hedged ("I think maybe…"), whether you stopped after beat one, and whether you actually answered the question that was asked. Every one you fumble is a chapter to revisit, and the sidebar is right there.

This is unpleasant and it is the single highest-return hour available to you before an interview.

Golden rules

  1. Answer in three beats: definition, consequence, experience. Most candidates stop at one.
  2. "I do not know — here is the nearest thing I do know, and what I would read" beats a bluff every time. Bluffing invalidates everything else you said.
  3. In live coding they are watching how you get unstuck, not whether you are quick.
  4. Restate the problem and ask about edge cases before typing.
  5. Think out loud. Silence cannot be given credit.
  6. Working and ugly, then improved, beats elegant and broken.
  7. Take the hint. Refusing help is the worst signal in the room.
  8. Every Italian salary figure is RAL, over 13 or 14 mensilità. Compare RAL with RAL, and verify current market data yourself.
  9. Ask which CCNL and which livello. It determines minimums, holidays and notice.
  10. Always have questions. Deployment frequency and code review tell you the most.
  11. Prepare three stories: a success, a failure, a disagreement.
  12. You are also interviewing them. A job you cannot learn in is a year you do not get back.

One last thing

You have read twenty-three chapters covering every line of that advert. That is more preparation than the large majority of applicants will do, and it will show — not because you will recite any of it, but because you will be able to have a conversation about the system rather than answer a quiz about it.

Nobody is hired for knowing everything. People are hired for being obviously able to learn, and obviously honest about what they do not yet know. Every chapter here has tried to give you the second thing as well as the first.
