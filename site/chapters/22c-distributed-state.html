Distributed state: replication, conflict and offline

Chapter 22b split one application into several services, each still owning one copy of its data. This chapter is about the harder thing: the same data existing in more than one place at once — two datacentres, three plants, forty tablets in vans — and every one of them able to change it.

Why this chapter exists

A whole class of advert asks for this in one line and does not blink: "architetture distribuite, sincronizzazione multi-master, consistenza dei dati, alta disponibilità, gestione di modalità degradate (offline)". Tracking and dispatching, field service, warehouse and plant systems, anything with a van or a handheld in it. Until now this site could answer distributed with microservices and queues, which is a different subject: 22 and 22b are about messages between services, and this is about the same fact stored twice.

It is also the topic where the honest answer scores highest, because the field has no clean one. Every technique here is a trade, all of them lose something, and an interviewer who has run such a system is listening for whether you know what you are giving up. A candidate who says "eventual consistency" with confidence and cannot say what a user sees during the gap has memorised the wrong half.

One fact, two copies

🧒 Simple

You and your brother keep a shopping list. There is one notebook on the kitchen table, and whoever wants to add something walks to the kitchen and writes it. Nothing ever goes wrong, but if the kitchen is locked nobody can add anything at all.

So you each take a copy of the list in your pocket, and promise to compare them in the evening. Now you can both write whenever you like — and now there is a new problem that did not exist before. He crossed out milk and you wrote milk, two bottles, and this evening somebody has to decide what the list really says. There is no rule that is right in every case. That decision, made in code, in advance, is what this chapter is.

🎓 Professional

Start from the topology, because the vocabulary follows it. Single-writer — one primary that takes every write, with read replicas following it — is the default and by a distance the easiest to reason about. There is exactly one order of events, so conflicts cannot exist. What you buy instead is replication lag: a read replica is always a little behind, so a user who writes and immediately reads can be shown their own stale data. That is the read-your-own-writes problem, and the usual fix is to route a user's reads to the primary for a few seconds after they write.

Multi-master — several nodes each accepting writes, converging afterwards — buys two things that are worth real money: a site that keeps working when the link to head office is down, and writes served close to the person making them. It costs you the single order of events, and the moment that is gone, two nodes can legally accept contradictory changes to the same row. Conflict resolution is not an edge case in that design; it is the design.

CAP is the sentence everyone half-remembers, and it is narrower than it sounds. It says nothing at all about the normal day. It says that during a network partition you must choose: refuse writes on the side that cannot reach the others and stay consistent (CP), or accept them and reconcile later (AP). The refinement worth knowing is PACELC, which adds the part CAP omits — else, with no partition at all, you are still trading latency against consistency, because agreeing with a node in another city takes as long as the round trip does.

And eventual consistency is a promise about the end state, not about any moment in it: given no new writes, all copies converge. It does not say when, and it does not say what an operator sees in the meantime. Designing the meantime is the job.

Deciding who wins, before it happens

There are four honest strategies, and they are worth knowing in this order, because the order is roughly from worst to best.

Last write wins. Keep a timestamp, take the higher one. It is one column, it always terminates, and it silently discards the other change — a write that a user was told had succeeded. Worse, it depends on clocks, and clocks on separate machines disagree by seconds without anyone noticing. The diagram above is not contrived: an operator in Bologna cancels an order at 09:14:01 and a scanner in Modena marks it loaded at 09:14:02, and the truth of which happened first is not recoverable from those two numbers. Use it where losing an update is merely annoying — a display preference, a last-seen marker — and nowhere else.

Version vectors. Each node keeps a counter per node, and comparing two vectors tells you honestly whether one change happened after the other or whether they are concurrent. That is a real improvement over a timestamp, and it is worth being precise about what it buys: it detects a conflict reliably and decides nothing. Something still has to resolve it — automatically by a domain rule, or by writing both versions down and asking a human, which is what a dispatcher is for.

Data types that cannot conflict. Some structures merge with no decision at all, because merging is defined to be order-independent: a counter that only increases, a set you only add to, an append-only log of events. These are the CRDT family, and the idea behind them is available to you long before you reach for a library. If what you replicate is "scanned parcel X at 10:04" rather than "parcel count is now 37", two sites can both write and the merge is a union with no loser. Modelling in events rather than in current state is the single biggest lever in this chapter.

Owning the key. The best conflict is the one that is structurally impossible. Give each site, van or device sole authority over a partition — its own orders, its own id range, its own set of rows — and replicate everything else read-only. Nobody else writes those rows, so nobody can disagree about them. Most systems described as multi-master in an advert are actually this, and proposing it is usually the senior answer: you keep the availability and you never build the resolver.

The two identifier traps

A database-generated IDENTITY or SERIAL cannot be the identity of a record created offline: the row does not exist yet, the number is not available, and when two vans reconnect they both want 1041. The fix is a client-generated identifier — a Guid, ideally a sequential one like a UUIDv7 or ULID so it does not fragment your clustered index. Generate it on the device, before the record has ever been near a server, and it stays the same forever.

The second trap is the human-facing number. Nobody dispatches consignment 9f2c-…; they read a short reference aloud over the phone. That is a display code, it is not the identity, and it should be allocated by the owning site out of a range reserved for it (MO-, BO-) or assigned centrally on sync. Confusing the two is how a system ends up with duplicate order numbers that are also different orders.

The sync endpoint

Almost all of this lands in one place: the endpoint a reconnecting client posts its backlog to. It is worth writing out, because every idea above shows up in it. 

// Accepts one change from a client that may have been offline for hours,
// may be sending it for the second time, and may be wrong about the world.
app.MapPut("/api/consignments/{id:guid}", async (
    Guid id,
    ConsignmentChange change,        // carries ClientVersion and ChangeId
    AppDb db,
    CancellationToken ct) =>
{
    // 1. Replay protection. The same change id has one effect, forever.
    if (await db.AppliedChanges.AnyAsync(a => a.ChangeId == change.ChangeId, ct))
    {
        return Results.Ok(await db.Consignments.FindAsync([id], ct));
    }

    var current = await db.Consignments.FindAsync([id], ct);

    // 2. Created offline: the client already knows its own id.
    if (current is null)
    {
        current = Consignment.FromChange(id, change);
        db.Consignments.Add(current);
    }
    else if (current.Version != change.ClientVersion)
    {
        // 3. Somebody else changed it while this client was away.
        var outcome = ConflictPolicy.Resolve(current, change);

        if (outcome.Kind is Resolution.NeedsAHuman)
        {
            db.ConflictQueue.Add(outcome.ToRecord(id, change));
            await db.SaveChangesAsync(ct);
            return Results.Conflict(new { current, yours = change, needsReview = true });
        }

        current.ApplyMerged(outcome);
    }
    else
    {
        current.Apply(change);
    }

    current.Version++;
    db.AppliedChanges.Add(new AppliedChange(change.ChangeId, DateTimeOffset.UtcNow));
    await db.SaveChangesAsync(ct);

    return Results.Ok(current);   // the server's truth goes back, always
});
Line by line
  • PUT with the id in the URL, not POST to a collection — because the client already knows the id it created, which makes the call idempotent by shape. This is the practical payoff of chapter 13's distinction rather than a point of etiquette.
  • ChangeId and the AppliedChanges table — the idempotency key. A tablet on a bad connection will send the same change twice: once before the response was lost, once on replay. Recording ids that have been applied turns "at least once" delivery into "exactly once" effect, and it is the same pattern as the consumer side of chapter 22. Note it must be written in the same transaction as the change, or a crash between the two reintroduces the duplicate.
  • current.Version != change.ClientVersion — optimistic concurrency, and the actual conflict detector. The client says which version it was editing; if the row has moved on, its change was authored against a world that no longer exists. In EF Core this is normally a [Timestamp] / rowversion column, and here it is explicit because the client has to carry it across the gap.
  • ConflictPolicy.Resolve as a named, testable thing — not an if buried in the handler. The resolution rule is domain knowledge that somebody in the business decided, it will change, and it is the part a reviewer will want tests for.
  • NeedsAHuman and ConflictQueue — the branch that most designs omit and most operations teams eventually demand. Some conflicts have no correct automatic answer, and a queue a dispatcher can work through is a feature, not an admission of failure.
  • 409 Conflict carrying both versions — so the device can show the user what happened rather than just failing. A sync that silently drops the driver's change is how field staff stop trusting the application, and that is a harder problem to fix than the code.
  • return Results.Ok(current) on every path, including the replay — the server's copy is the answer to every sync call. The client overwrites itself with it. Any design where the client keeps its own version after a sync has two truths again.

The offline client, and being honest on screen

On the device, the shape is the outbox from chapter 22, moved one tier out. Writes go to a local store — IndexedDB in a browser, SQLite in a mobile or desktop app — and to a queue in the same local transaction. The UI reads the local store, so it is instant and works with no network. A replayer drains the queue whenever there is a connection. 

// The replay loop. Boring on purpose: order, idempotency, and no surprises.
async function drainOutbox(): Promise<void> {
  if (draining || !navigator.onLine) return;
  draining = true;

  try {
    for (const item of await outbox.readAllInOrder()) {
      const res = await fetch(`/api/consignments/${item.entityId}`, {
        method:  'PUT',
        headers: { 'Content-Type': 'application/json' },
        body:    JSON.stringify(item.change),
      });

      if (res.ok) {
        await local.replaceFromServer(await res.json());
        await outbox.remove(item.id);
      } else if (res.status === 409) {
        await local.markConflicted(item.entityId, await res.json());
        await outbox.remove(item.id);            // resolved, not retried
      } else if (res.status >= 400 && res.status < 500) {
        await outbox.moveToDeadLetter(item);     // it will never be accepted
      } else {
        break;                                   // server or network: stop, keep order
      }
    }
  } finally {
    draining = false;
  }
}

window.addEventListener('online', drainOutbox);
Line by line
  • if (draining) — a single-flight guard. Two drains at once send the same item twice and reorder the rest; the online event and a timer will both fire during a flaky reconnect, so this is not hypothetical.
  • readAllInOrder — the queue is ordered, and order is part of the meaning. "Assigned to Marco" then "cancelled" is a different day from "cancelled" then "assigned to Marco".
  • break on 5xx or a network error — stop the whole drain, do not skip ahead. Continuing past a failure is what turns a temporary outage into permanently reordered history.
  • 4xx moves to a dead letter instead of retrying — the server has said this will never be accepted, so retrying it forever blocks everything behind it. Same reasoning, same name, as the dead-letter queue in chapter 22.
  • replaceFromServer — the local copy is overwritten by the response, not merged with it. The server's answer is the truth; anything else and the device slowly drifts away from everyone else.
  • markConflicted — the conflict becomes visible state on the device, so the screen can tell the driver their change was overruled and by what. Removing the item without showing anything is the single most damaging shortcut available here.
  • navigator.onLine is only a hint — it reports whether there is a network interface, not whether your API is reachable. Hotel wi-fi with a captive portal is "online". Treat it as a cheap trigger, and let the actual fetch decide.
Say what the user is looking at

A degraded mode is a user-interface requirement before it is an architecture one. The screen must say which of three worlds it is in — live, working offline with n changes pending, or reconnecting — and it must be honest about the age of the data: "positions as of 09:12, not updating", not a map that looks exactly like a live one. An operator who cannot tell the difference will dispatch a van that was reassigned twenty minutes ago, and the fault for that lands on the software.

The same rule applies to actions. Some things are safe to do offline and reconcile later — notes, status updates, signatures. Some are not: allocating the last unit of stock, taking a payment, assigning a slot somebody else may also be assigning. Disable those, say why, and you have converted an unsolvable consistency problem into a product decision, which is the only place it can actually be solved.

High availability, and the words underneath it

Alta disponibilità in an advert means the system survives losing a machine, and the first question to ask back is always the same two numbers. RTO is how long you may be down; RPO is how much data you may lose. A business that answers "zero and zero" has not been asked properly, and the answers drive everything else, because they decide whether replication is synchronous.

Synchronous replication — the primary waits for a replica to confirm before it acknowledges — gives an RPO of zero and charges you the round trip on every commit, on the good days as well as the bad. Asynchronous is fast and loses whatever had not shipped when the primary died. Both are legitimate; the mistake is not knowing which one you are running, which is depressingly common.

Then failover. Automatic failover needs a tiebreaker, because two nodes that cannot see each other will both conclude the other has died and both promote themselves: split-brain, and now you have two primaries accepting conflicting writes with no plan for merging them. The answer is a quorum — an odd number of voters, so a majority exists — and fencing, so the demoted node is actually prevented from writing rather than politely asked. This is why clusters come in threes and why a two-node "highly available" pair is often less safe than one node.

Concretely, in the engines this course uses: SQL Server does this with Always On availability groups, synchronous or asynchronous per replica, with readable secondaries. PostgreSQL ships streaming replication with a synchronous option and, since version 10, logical replication — which is the building block people mean when they say multi-master on Postgres, usually via an extension, and which replicates row changes rather than the physical log. Neither engine merges conflicts for you in that mode. That decision comes back to your code, which is the whole reason this chapter exists.

Do not build this by accident

Every technique here costs testing, operations and a class of bug that is hard to reproduce. The correct default remains a single primary with replicas and a proper backup, and most systems never outgrow it. Multi-master is earned by a specific requirement — a site that must keep working when the line drops, a regulator, a latency number, vans with no signal — and the right first response to "we need multi-master" is "which of those is it?"

Saying that in an interview is not ducking the question. It is the answer somebody who has maintained one of these gives, and the follow-up you will get — "all right, the vans, how would you do it?" — is the conversation you wanted.

Interview questions

"Che cos'è la consistenza eventuale?"

A guarantee that if writing stops, every copy converges on the same value. It promises nothing about any particular moment, which is the half people leave out: two users can legitimately see different answers at the same instant, and the design question is what each of them is shown and what they are allowed to do about it. Contrast it with strong consistency, where every read sees the latest write and you pay for that in latency or availability.

"Explain CAP without the triangle."

It only applies during a network partition. When one part of the system cannot reach another, you either refuse writes on the isolated side and stay consistent, or accept them and reconcile afterwards. There is no third option and no normal-operation trade-off in CAP at all. The part worth adding is PACELC: with no partition you are still choosing between latency and consistency, because agreement between distant nodes costs a round trip.

"Two sites both edit the same order while the link is down. How do you resolve it?"

First say what you would do to avoid the question: partition ownership so one site owns that order and the others hold it read-only, which removes the conflict rather than resolving it. Where genuine concurrent writes are required, detect with version vectors or per-field versions rather than timestamps — clocks on separate machines disagree — and then resolve by a domain rule, by merging at field level where the fields are independent, or by queueing it for a human when there is no correct automatic answer. Last-write-wins is acceptable only where silently losing somebody's change is acceptable.

"How does a client that has been offline for four hours sync?"

Writes went to a local store and a local outbox in one transaction, with client-generated ids so nothing depended on the server. On reconnect the outbox drains in order, one change at a time, each carrying an idempotency key the server records so a retry has no second effect and each carrying the version it was authored against, so the server can detect that the world moved. The server's response replaces the local copy, a 409 becomes visible conflict state on the device, and a 5xx stops the drain so ordering is preserved.

"Why are client-generated GUIDs the usual answer, and what do they cost?"

Because a record created with no network has to have an identity immediately, and a database-generated sequence cannot supply one without a round trip — and two devices would both claim the same next number. The cost is index behaviour: random GUIDs scatter inserts across a clustered index and fragment it, so use a time-ordered variant such as UUIDv7 or ULID, or NEWSEQUENTIALID. Keep the human-readable reference as a separate display field allocated by the owning site, because people read those aloud.

"What is split-brain, and how do you prevent it?"

Two nodes lose sight of each other, each assumes the other is dead, and both promote themselves to primary — so both accept writes that now have to be merged, which is exactly the situation the cluster existed to avoid. Prevention is a quorum: an odd number of voters so only a majority side can elect a leader, plus fencing so a demoted node is physically stopped from writing. It is why clusters come in threes, and why a two-node pair with automatic failover can be less safe than a single node.

"RTO and RPO — and which one does asynchronous replication affect?"

RTO is how long the system may be unavailable; RPO is how much data may be lost. Asynchronous replication affects RPO: whatever had not reached the replica when the primary died is gone, so the RPO is roughly the replication lag. Synchronous replication takes that to zero and charges the round trip on every commit instead. Neither is a backup, and a replica that faithfully replicates a mistaken DELETE is the demonstration.
