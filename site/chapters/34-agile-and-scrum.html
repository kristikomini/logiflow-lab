Agile, Scrum and the daily rhythm

Every advert says metodologie Agile. No course teaches it, so most juniors arrive having read the Wikipedia page and are then quietly bewildered for a month — not by the theory, but by what you are supposed to say at nine o'clock every morning. This chapter is mostly about that.

Why this chapter exists

"Conoscenza delle metodologie di sviluppo (Agile, Scrum)" is on nearly every advert from the big consultancies hiring in Italy — NTT Data, Accenture, Reply, Engineering — and on most product-company adverts too. It is never the reason you get hired, and looking lost in your first sprint is absolutely a reason people conclude a junior is not working out. It is also the cheapest chapter here to actually master.

What Agile actually claims

🧒 Simple

Two ways to draw a picture for your friend.

One: they describe it once, you go away for a month, and you show them the finished drawing. If you misheard "cat" as "hat", the whole month is wasted.

Two: you sketch for an hour and show them. "No, a cat." You fix it and show them again. You still take a month, but you are never more than an hour away from finding out you are wrong.

That is the entire idea. Not "no planning" — just never go a long time without checking.

🎓 Professional

The 2001 manifesto states four preferences — individuals and interactions over processes and tools, working software over comprehensive documentation, customer collaboration over contract negotiation, responding to change over following a plan — each phrased as "while there is value in the items on the right, we value the items on the left more". That clause is routinely dropped, which is how "we're agile" came to mean "we don't write anything down".

Underneath, the engineering argument is about the cost of being wrong. A long feedback loop means errors are discovered late, when they are expensive. Everything else — short iterations, continuous integration, automated tests, small releases — is machinery for shortening that loop. Which is why testing and CI/CD are not separate topics from Agile; they are what make it physically possible.

The daily stand-up, scripted

🧒 Simple

Every morning, everyone stands in a circle for a few minutes and says: what I did, what I am doing, and whether I am stuck. It is short because standing up is uncomfortable — that is genuinely why.

It is not the teacher checking your homework. It is the team making sure nobody is quietly stuck on something someone else already solved.

🎓 Professional

Fifteen minutes, same time, same place, for the developers — not a status report to a manager. The purpose is coordination and surfacing blockers, and the useful contribution is the third part, not the first two.

The shape that always works:

Ieri  — ho finito la validazione su POST /orders,
        la PR è aperta e aspetta review.
Oggi  — prendo il ticket 412, l'export CSV.
Bloc. — nessuno.
        (oppure: mi serve l'accesso al DB di collaudo,
         chi me lo può dare?)

Three sentences. Say the ticket number, not a vague area. And if you are blocked, say so on the first day, not the third — a junior sitting silently stuck for two days is the single most damaging thing you can do, and it is entirely avoidable. Nobody has ever been thought less of for asking on day one; plenty have been for asking on day three.

⚠ The stand-up anti-patterns you will see
  • The status report. Everyone talks to the manager instead of each other. Tell: nobody responds to anybody.
  • The design discussion. Two people go twenty minutes deep while six wait. The correct move — and it is a good look, even as a junior — is "lo prendiamo offline?"
  • "Same as yesterday." Said three days running. That is a blocker with the word "blocker" removed.

Story points, velocity, and the trap

🎓 Professional

Points measure relative size — complexity, uncertainty and volume — not hours. Usually a Fibonacci-ish scale (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13) because the gaps encode the fact that big estimates are less precise. A 13 is not a number, it is a message: this is too big, split it.

Why not hours? Because humans are bad at absolute duration and reasonably good at comparison — "this is about twice that one" is a judgement people make well. And because an estimate in hours is heard as a commitment, and then defended rather than revised.

Velocity is points completed per sprint, averaged. It exists to forecast this team's next sprint. It is not a productivity metric, it does not compare teams (a point means something different in each one), and the moment it becomes a target the numbers inflate and the measure stops working — Goodhart's law, live.

TermWhat it actually means
Definition of ReadyWhen a ticket may enter a sprint: understood, estimated, acceptance criteria written, dependencies known
Definition of DoneWhen a ticket may leave: merged, tested, reviewed, documented, deployed to the agreed environment. Agreed once, applies to everything
Acceptance criteriaThe checks that decide if it works. Often "Given / When / Then"
Refinement (grooming)Mid-sprint session where the team clarifies and estimates upcoming items

What you will actually find

🎓 Professional

Very few teams run textbook Scrum, and the honest term for what most run is "Scrum-but": sprints and stand-ups, but the Product Owner is the client's project manager, scope changes mid-sprint, and the retrospective is the first thing dropped when things get busy.

Kanban is the common alternative and often the better fit for support and maintenance work: no sprints, a visible board, and a work-in-progress limit — a column may hold at most N items, so finishing something becomes the only way to start something. Plenty of teams run a hybrid: sprint planning for features, a Kanban lane for incidents.

In consultancy — a very large share of the Italian market — there is a second reality on top: you may sit at the client's site, follow the client's process, and be measured on billable hours as well as sprint goals. That is worth asking about in an interview rather than discovering in month two.

Try it yourself

Run one two-week sprint on your own study, in the real tooling. Azure DevOps is free for small teams and is what many companies in the region use: 

# dev.azure.com → new project → Boards
# create work items for the chapters you still need, estimate each in points,
# pull two weeks' worth into a sprint, and move them across the board

At the end, do the part everyone skips: compare what you planned with what you finished, and write three lines — what went well, what did not, one thing to change. That is a retrospective, and having actually run one is worth more in an interview than being able to define it. It also gives you a real number for your own velocity, which will be lower than you expect and is the most useful thing you will learn all fortnight.

Golden rules

  1. Agile is short feedback loops. Every ceremony exists to shorten one.
  2. Say the ticket number, not "the API thing".
  3. Raise a blocker on day one. Silence is the expensive option.
  4. The stand-up is for the team, not for the manager. Fifteen minutes, standing.
  5. "Let's take it offline" is a contribution, not an interruption.
  6. Points are relative size, not hours. A 13 means "split this".
  7. Velocity forecasts your team's next sprint. It never compares teams and is never a target.
  8. Definition of Done is agreed once and applies to everything, or it means nothing.
  9. Scope frozen inside the sprint; new requests go to the backlog. That is the whole deal.
  10. Review is about the product; retrospective is about the team. Keep them apart.
  11. A retrospective with no action item was a chat.
  12. Agile is not "no documentation". It is less documentation that nobody reads.

Interview questions

"Hai lavorato con metodologie Agile?"

If you have not worked in a team yet, do not bluff — describe what you have done and what you understand. "I have run my study as two-week sprints on Azure Boards with estimates and a retrospective, so I know the rhythm and the tooling, and I understand the roles. I have not yet done it with a Product Owner and a real client." Concrete, honest, and it beats a recited definition every time.

"What is the point of the daily stand-up?"

Coordination and surfacing blockers — not reporting status upward. Then show you know how it goes wrong: it becomes a status round where nobody responds to anybody, or two people design for twenty minutes while six wait. Say that the useful part is the blocker, and that you say it early.

"Why estimate in points rather than hours?"

People compare well and predict absolute duration badly, and an hours estimate is heard as a commitment and then defended instead of revised. Points capture complexity and uncertainty as well as size. Then add the caution about velocity as a target — knowing why a metric is dangerous reads as experience.

"You realise mid-sprint that your estimate was badly wrong. What do you do?"

Say so immediately, at the next stand-up, with what you now know and what you would propose — split the ticket, drop a sub-case, or ask for help. Explicitly reject the alternative: quietly working late to protect the estimate hides the problem until the review, when it is too late for anyone to act. They are testing whether bad news travels fast with you.

"What would you change about how your last team worked?"

Answer it as a retrospective, not a complaint: one specific thing, why it cost the team, and what you would propose instead. "Tickets were often too big to finish in a sprint, so the burndown was flat until the last two days — I would push for splitting anything estimated above an 8." Blaming individuals fails this question; naming a process problem passes it.
